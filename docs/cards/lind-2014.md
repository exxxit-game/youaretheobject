# lind-2014 — You said "grey", you heard "green" (real-time speech exchange)

- paper: lind-2014.txt
- citation: Lind, Hall, Breidegard, Balkenius & Johansson (2014). Speakers' acceptance of real-time speech exchange indicates that we use auditory feedback to specify the meaning of what we say. Psychological Science (OnlineFirst 28 April 2014), doi:10.1177/0956797614529797. Text: the authors' OnlineFirst PDF with the Supplemental Online Material, from Lund University Cognitive Science (lucs.lu.se); page numbers are the OnlineFirst pages 1–8 and SOM pages DS1–DS3.

| Participants | 83 recruited, mostly Lund students, Swedish native speakers; 78 analysed | "Eighty-three participants (44 female, 39 male; mean age = 23.7 years, SD = 4.1), most of whom were students, were recruited at Lund University." | 3 |

| Procedure: question | After each swap (and 4 control trials) the screen asks "What did you say?" | "Directly following each manipulated trial (c), the question "What did you say?" appeared on the screen and remained until the participant verbalized an answer." | 2 |
| Equipment: headset | Sound-isolating earmuff headset; own voice played back loud to mask the air-conducted voice | "Participants wore a specially constructed sound-isolated circumaural headset" | 3 |

| Measure: detection | Structured interview afterwards, then the reveal; trials after a first detection dropped | "we conducted a structured posttest interview, asking increasingly specific questions about the participants' experience of the experiment" | 3 |

## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: about 10 minutes. Fits.
- Task/tension: a fast colour-naming task with mild time pressure; the surprise is entirely in the reveal ("you said grey, and you believed you said green").
- Within-person reveal: very strong. The system has both recordings: what the player really said (which they never heard) and what was played to them. Playing the two back side by side is a reveal of the player's own voice and their own report.
- Works if the player expects tricks: badly. Detection depends on not suspecting; in the paper, everything after a first detection was discarded. A player who knows the game manipulates things will listen for it. Naivety cannot be assumed.
- Live players needed: 1 (no other people). This is a voice card, not a group card.
- Space tier: seated.
- VR or mixed reality: neither matters for the original (a screen in a recording studio). VR gives full control of the visual display; mixed reality adds nothing.
- Quest 3 feasibility: doubtful as is. The original masked the player's own air-conducted voice with sound-isolating earmuffs and loud feedback, with 8 ms delay and swaps timed within 5–20 ms. Quest 3's open-ear speakers cannot block the player's real voice, and the Quest Browser's microphone-to-speaker delay is unknown here (not measured; likely well above 8 ms). It could only work with closed headphones plugged into the headset and a measured delay inside the 5–20 ms window. The microphone also needs the player's permission. Measure first; if the delay is too long, the room is not offered.
- Replication: one study, 78 Swedish speakers, one lab. No independent replication is reported in the paper.
- Ethics: deception (covert manipulation of one's own voice), with a full debrief before consent, as in the paper. Voice privacy: the recorded words should stay on the headset, be used only for the swap and the reveal, and be deleted afterwards; nothing is sent.
- Verdict: room (voice, one player) — 10 minutes, a fully automatic stimulus and a striking within-person reveal, but it stands or falls on low audio delay and masking the player's own voice, which the Quest's open speakers probably cannot do; measure before building.
