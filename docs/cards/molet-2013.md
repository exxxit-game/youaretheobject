## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: about 6–8 min — fits.
- Task/tension: low — visit two houses, pick a favourite; the music looks incidental.
- Within-person reveal: strong — the player's own split of time between houses and the first house chosen, set against where the music played; in the paper nobody attributed the choice to the music.
- Works if the player expects tricks: weak to moderate — the music is the only difference between visits, and the "recording for another study" cover will not survive a psychology game; a suspicious player may choose the other house on purpose. Making the music diegetic (a radio in one house) helps only a little.
- Space tier: seated (desktop computer with keyboard and mouse).
- VR or mixed reality: VR — two distinct virtual houses; mixed reality has no second house.
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy — two houses, thumbstick or teleport movement (closest to the arrow keys), music through the headset; time per house logged automatically (the paper coded it from video). Moving the task from a monitor into a headset is a change of medium, not of procedure.
- Replication: single lab, 15–16 per experiment, huge effects (85% and 96%) and a text/table mismatch. Later conceptual replications (read in childs-2017, papers folder): food (Astur 2014), points (Astur 2016 — only with a biased room assignment) and money (Childs 2017, two sites, N = 57: preference on day 1, gone 24 h later). So the effect is real but probably smaller and short-lived.
- Ethics: good — music reward; the dissonant version is mildly unpleasant; keep volume moderate and allow skipping.
- Verdict: room — short, ethical, automatically measured, with a personal reveal ("you spent most of your time where the music was"); main risk is that players spot the manipulation, and the original effect size is probably inflated.
