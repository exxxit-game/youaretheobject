## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: 170 s per block — a 3–4 min interlude for one block; the paper's two blocks with a 5-min gap would be about 10 min.
- Task/tension: none — the player watches faces on a screen; the surprise is the player's own yawn.
- Within-person reveal: yes, but only by self-report: "did you yawn?" About 4 in 10 do. The paper's real finding (a person in the room stops it) is a question about the player's real room, compared across players.
- Works if the player expects tricks: fairly well — knowing the topic does not stop yawns, and the paper notes that even thinking about yawning can trigger one; some players may suppress it deliberately.
- Space tier: seated (swivel chair, no walking).
- VR or mixed reality: VR matches the original (virtual screen 20 m away). Mixed reality is a natural, new variant: the paper's point is that the real room dominates the virtual one, so showing the clips on the player's real wall could be tested — a different experiment.
- Quest 3 feasibility: showing the clips is trivial (the original clips come from the authors on request; rights unclear — new consented clips may be needed). The measure is the problem: Quest 3 has no face tracking, so yawns cannot be detected automatically; only a button/self-report (the paper also accepted self-report when video failed). Microphone or head-pitch detection of yawns is unvalidated.
- Replication: contagious yawning to these clips is long-established (about 40%); the researcher-presence effect repeats the same lab's earlier study; the four virtual social cues (webcam, avatars) all had no effect. Each experiment had only about 20 people, and yawning rates varied 19–45% between experiments.
- Ethics: none special.
- Verdict: interlude — a harmless 3-minute moment where the player may catch a yawn, but the only measure on Quest 3 is self-report and the paper's key effect needs a real person in the room, which a game cannot control.
