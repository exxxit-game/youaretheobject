## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: the survey answer takes seconds to a minute (duration not stated); a VR version is a 1–3 minute scene.
- Task/tension: one hard, personal choice — plug in or not. In VR the irony is strong: the player is already inside a machine of experiences.
- Within-person reveal: weak — one answer per person, no trick to uncover. The only "reveal" is showing the player how others chose (29% / 53% / 89% in the paper).
- Works if the player expects tricks: yes — nothing is hidden; it measures a stated choice, not a fooled perception. But the VR setting itself may push answers (the player chose to be in VR), which is a new confound, not a replication.
- Space tier: seated (an online text survey; nothing in the procedure needs movement).
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy; the choice is logged automatically. A Likert slider is also easy.
- Replication: the paper is two online experiments by one team; it replicates the "most refuse the machine" direction of Weijers and De Brigard but not the exact rate (29% vs. 16%). Per-cell groups are small (about 35 per condition).
- What it would take to call it science: keep the paper's between-subjects design — randomly assign each player one vignette (machine / experience pill / functioning pill, and optionally the Exp. 2 "prevent a loss" versions), use the paper's exact wording and yes/no question, pre-register the predicted order (machine < experience pill < functioning pill) and a sample of several hundred per arm, and report the VR medium as a deviation. Never show other players' results before the answer.
- Ethics: none special; no deception. Avoid implying the player should leave or stay in VR.
- Verdict: interlude — a one-question moment that measures cleanly and can replicate the 29/53/89 order across many players, but it is a single choice, not a room.
