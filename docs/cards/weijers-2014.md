## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: a paper vignette and one tick; under a few minutes (duration not stated). A VR version is a 1–3 minute scene.
- Task/tension: one personal choice, plug in or not. The Stranger version is already a scene: a stranger sits down next to you in a hovertube and tells you about his offer. In VR the irony is strong, because the player is already inside a machine of experiences.
- Within-person reveal: none. Each person answered one scenario, so the effects (16% → 34% → 52% → 55%) are between groups. The only reveal is "you vs other players" after the answer.
- Works if the player expects tricks: yes. Nothing is hidden, and it records a stated choice. But a player who has seen another version will answer differently (see chandler-2015), so each player must get one version only, ever.
- Space tier: seated (a paper survey; nothing needs movement).
- VR or mixed reality: VR suits the Stranger scene (a hovertube carriage with Boris beside you). The original is a paper text, so any staging is a change. Mixed reality adds nothing to a thought experiment, and seeing one's real room may itself act as a "reality is the status quo" cue.
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy. One vignette (read aloud or shown), one two-option choice logged automatically, optional spoken or typed reason. The written-reason coding (imaginative resistance) cannot be automated reliably.
- Replication: one paper, all students, about 80 per scenario, one-tailed tests. The status-quo manipulation was significant in a 2011 run (p = .051, one-tailed) but not in 2012. Hindriks & Douven (2018) re-ran a short machine vignette and got 29% (10 of 35), against Weijers' 16% for Nozick's text. They quote Weijers' 13 of 79 correctly, which this full text confirms.
- Compared with hindriks-douven-2018: that paper varies what is offered (machine vs experience pill vs functioning pill: 29 / 53 / 89% yes) and good vs bad framing (small effect). This paper varies who decides and what counts as the status quo (Nozick 16%, Self 34%, Stranger 52%, no status quo 55%). Both find most people refusing in the first-person machine framing. Only Weijers shows that a majority accepts once the choice is made for a stranger without a status quo. The two designs could be offered as one interlude with random assignment across the combined arms.
- Ethics: none special; no deception. Avoid implying the player should stay in or leave VR.
- Verdict: interlude — a one-choice scene whose between-groups framing effect (16% → 55%) could be replicated across many players, especially the VR-native Stranger version; but it is a single answer with no personal reveal, not a room.
