## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: the questionnaire had no time limit (8 picture pairs, likely under 10 min); looking at the ambiguous object takes seconds, its reveal about a minute.
- Task/tension: the picture questionnaire is dry; the ambiguous object gives a strong "how is that possible?" moment but no task.
- Within-person reveal: for the object, yes — walk around it or rotate it, the exhibition sequence in the paper is a ready script. For the pictures, only "it was symmetric after all".
- Works if the player expects tricks: unknown — this paper has no data on viewers who know the trick; the object reports are anecdotal.
- Space tier: seated (the questionnaire study). Viewing the object from its special viewpoint is standing in place.
- Quest 3 feasibility: the models are easy to build in VR, but the illusion depends on one special viewpoint, and a headset always gives two-eye depth and head-motion parallax, which may break it (the paper notes two eyes give depth when an object is seen directly). It must be tried in the headset before any design. No automatic measure in the paper; a game would have to ask a question (e.g. which way the arrow points).
- Replication: the picture study is 16 people in one lab with statistics the authors call optimistic; a published commentary (Sawada & Dvoeglazova, 2023, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience) disputes its design and conclusion. There are no observer data on the ambiguous objects themselves.
- Ethics: none special.
- Verdict: interlude — a striking look-and-reveal object, not an experiment with measured data; first prove in the headset that it survives two-eye viewing.
