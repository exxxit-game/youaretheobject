# wen-2015 — The helper you never noticed (secret assistance makes you feel more in control)

- paper: wen-2015.txt
- citation: Wen, Yamashita & Asama (2015). The sense of agency during continuous action: performance is more important than action-feedback association. PLoS ONE, 10(4), e0125226. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0125226 (open access, CC BY)
- status: read

## Facts
| What | Value | Quote (verbatim from the .txt) | Page |
|---|---|---|---|
| Participants | 17 students, mean age 25.5 | "A total of 17 students with normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity participated in the experiment." | 4 |
| Procedure | Steer a moving dot into a target square with left/right keys | "The participants were instructed to direct a moving dot into a square as quickly as possible by pressing the left and right keys" | 1 |
| Procedure: delay | Keys act after 100, 400 or 700 ms (varies by trial) | "There were three possible delays (100, 400, and 700 ms) between the participant's key press and the dot's response" | 4 |
| Procedure: secret assistance | In half the trials, commands that would turn the dot away from the target were silently ignored | "commands that caused the dot to move away from the target location were ignored (the assisted condition, Fig 2)" | 5 |
| Procedure: not told | Participants were told only that the dot would sometimes be delayed or out of their control | "Participants were not notified about the computer assistance" | 5 |
| Procedure: trials | 60 trials: 10 per delay × assistance | "each participant completed 60 trials comprising 10 trials involving each delay duration (100, 400, and 700 ms) in each assistance condition" | 5 |
| Measure | After each trial, rate felt control 1–9 | "participants rated the extent to which they felt that the dot was under their control on a 9-point scale" | 4 |
| Space | Seated at a monitor, keyboard and mouse | "seated on a chair positioned 50 cm away from a 27-inch LCD monitor" | 5 |
| Duration | About 30 minutes | "The experiment lasted for 30 min, on average." | 5 |
| Main result | Higher felt control with assistance | "participants reported greater control in the assisted condition relative to the self-control condition" | 5 |
| Main result: when | No difference at 100 ms delay; significant at 400 and 700 ms | "rating scores did not differ between the control and assisted conditions when the delay was 100 ms" | 6 |
| Main result: actual control | Real control was about 42% lower in assisted trials | "their actual control was reduced by approximately 42% (averaged across all delay conditions)" | 13 |
| Main result: nobody noticed | One person noticed; everyone rated assisted trials higher | "only one participant noticed that the dot did not respond to a large proportion of commands" | 13 |
| Main result: all participants | Every participant felt more control with the helper | "all of the participants reported higher levels of control in the assisted condition relative to the self-control condition" | 13 |
| Background | Earlier game study (Metcalfe & Greene): agency follows performance, even under lenient "magic" scoring | "Metcalfe and Greene found that participants' judgment of self-agency was greatly influenced by their performance in the game" | 13 |

## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: 60 short trials, about 30 minutes in the original; a game version with fewer trials (for example 24–36) could fit 10–15 minutes, though the per-player difference would be noisier.
- Task/tension: a small steering game with a target and a clock; mild tension from the delays.
- Within-person reveal: strong. Every player has both conditions, and in the paper every participant rated assisted trials higher; the game can show "on these trials the game threw away 40% of your commands, and you felt most in control".
- Works if the player expects tricks: partly. Only one of 17 noticed the rule in the paper, but a player of a psychology game may suspect a helper; at short delays (100 ms) the effect vanished, so it depends on keeping the delays.
- Space tier: seated.
- VR or mixed reality: neither is needed for the original (a screen and keys); in VR the dot could become a steered object (a boat, a drone) driven by controller buttons or tilt, which keeps the logic but changes the task.
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy and automatic: delays, ignoring wrong commands, timing and a 1–9 rating are all simple. Rendering the 400–700 ms delays is trivial; the risk is that added headset latency blurs the 100 ms condition.
- Replication: a single study of 17; it agrees with an earlier game study (Metcalfe & Greene) cited in the paper. Later replications were not checked here (search was unavailable at this point).
- Ethics: mild deception (participants are not told about the assistance); a debrief explains it. Relevant to interface design (assistance and "dark" designs that make users feel in control).
- Verdict: interlude (pilot first): a short within-person agency illusion with a striking reveal (everyone felt more in control when the game secretly overrode them); one small study, so check replications before giving it a room.
