## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: not stated; about 60 quick pointing trials, likely 5–10 min.
- Task/tension: mild — "put this many where it belongs on the line"; no right answers shown, so the player cannot tell they are bending the scale.
- Within-person reveal: yes, partly. In this paper educated Americans were straight (linear) for number words and small dot sets but compressed (logarithmic) for 10–100 dots and for tone sequences, so one player can be shown their own two lines side by side: straight for words, bent for large dot clouds. The Mundurucu result (bent even for 1–10 and for words) is a group difference between cultures that the player cannot see in themselves; it can only be shown as the paper's data.
- Works if the player expects tricks: uncertain. A player who knows about the bend could try to spread large dot sets evenly; how well that works is not tested in the paper.
- Space tier: seated (a 25 cm line on a laptop screen, pointing; no movement).
- Quest 3 feasibility: good — a line in front of the player, dots and tones played by the headset, pointing with a controller ray or finger, every placement logged automatically. No eye tracking needed. Spoken Mundurucu/Portuguese words would be replaced by the player's own language, which matches the paper's "first language" condition.
- Replication: none inside the paper beyond the 7-person pencil control; the paper drew a published comment arguing against the logarithmic reading (Cantlon et al., with the authors' response in Science 323, 2009).
- Ethics: none special; present the Mundurucu with respect (a different number culture, not a deficit).
- Verdict: room — short, seated, fully automatic, and gives each player a real within-person contrast (words straight, large dot sets bent); the Amazonian comparison is group-level and can only be shown as context.
