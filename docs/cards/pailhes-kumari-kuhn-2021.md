## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: duration not stated; one trick is two touch rounds (well under a minute); three repetitions plus 0–100 ratings likely 3–5 min, so it fits inside a 5–15 min room.
- Task/tension: choose cards by touching them; tension comes from the magician's written prediction.
- Within-person reveal: yes — replay the player's own touches, then play the other routes: whichever cards they had touched, the 3rd card would have stayed. Softer than choice blindness, because players already rate their impact only moderate (about 53/100).
- Works if the player expects tricks: likely — participants were told the study was about magic tricks, and none in Exp. 1–2 saw through it even after three repetitions. Magic fans may know the Equivoque by name.
- Space tier: seated (participant sat at a café table facing the experimenter).
- Quest 3 feasibility: good — seated, four cards on a virtual table touched with hands or controllers, a virtual magician removes cards, both ratings on sliders (automatic). The original was live with a human; the NPC's keep/discard must look as casual. VR-specific weakness: in a game the computer can change a card at any moment, so the prediction reveal will impress less than live; the felt-impact rating before the reveal is the measure that matters. The word "touch" (not "pick" or "choose") must keep its ambiguity in every language.
- Replication: three experiments in this paper (cards twice, destinations once), Exp. 2 by a second, blind experimenter; no independent lab in this text.
- Ethics: none special; mild deception, explained by the reveal.
- Verdict: first-room candidate — the outcome is guaranteed for every player (an outcome force, not a probability), seated, a few minutes, robust to repetition and to "magic trick" framing, rated automatically; caveat: the felt control is only moderate.
