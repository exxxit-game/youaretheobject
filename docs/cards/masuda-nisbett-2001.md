## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: Study 1 as published is about 20–30 min (10 scenes with up to 2 min of talking each, plus 90 test items) — too long. Study 2 (24 pictures × 5 s with ratings, 2 min filler, 48-item test) is roughly 8–10 min and fits.
- Task/tension: low — watch a fish tank or rate animals; the only tension is the unannounced memory test at the end.
- Within-person reveal: weak. The headline result is a Japan–US group difference; one player cannot see "holistic" or "analytic" attention in themselves, and one session gives no valid personal label. What a player can see is their own drop in recognition when the background is swapped (in Study 2 both groups dropped, the Japanese more), which is a small, ordinary memory effect rather than the paper's point.
- Works if the player expects tricks: poorly. The recognition test only works as a surprise; a player of a psychology game will expect a memory test and study the whole scene, which changes exactly what is measured.
- Space tier: seated (fixed screen at 38 cm with a chin rest; no movement).
- Quest 3 feasibility: the scenes, the test and yes/no answers with reaction times are easy to build and log automatically (no eye tracking needed — the paper did not use it). The free-recall measure is not automatic: it needs speech recording, transcription and the paper's coding scheme (human coders, 94–97% agreement), so a faithful Study 1 cannot be scored by the game itself. Study 2 can.
- Replication: only within the paper (Study 2 and one follow-up, same lab); no independent replication reported in this paper.
- Ethics: no deception beyond the unannounced test; must not tell a player their "cultural thinking style" from one score.
- Verdict: interlude — Study 2's background-swap memory test fits 10 minutes, seated and fully automatic, but the paper's actual finding is a group difference between cultures that a single player cannot see in themselves, and the surprise test breaks once players expect it.
