## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: one forest-vs-town pair is 2 × (short stressor + 6 min exposure + questions), about 15–18 min; a single 6-minute exposure fits easily.
- Task/tension: none during exposure (sit and look); the serial subtraction gives a short, real task.
- Within-person reveal: weak to moderate — the game can show "after the town you made N more subtraction errors and rated yourself more tired", but the effects are small group averages and one player's difference is noisy.
- Works if the player expects tricks: poorly — everyone expects a forest to be calming, so mood ratings carry strong expectation; the subtraction-error measure is less exposed to this.
- Space tier: seated (fixed chair).
- VR or mixed reality: VR; the scene must replace the room. Mixed reality adds nothing to the original.
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy — 360° video on a sphere and in-headset rating sliders; subtraction answers by keypad or voice. Rating inside the headset and typed answers change the paper's instruments (paper questionnaires, spoken answers). Skin conductance and pulse are not available.
- Replication: the general claim (VR nature improves self-reported mood pre–post) recurs in most studies of a 2025 meta-analysis (Hubbard et al., 24 studies, mostly fair quality; abstract read), which also found no VR-vs-flat-screen difference, matching this paper. Browning et al. (2020, Frontiers, read in full in scratch) found VR forest only preserved positive affect, and negative affect fell in the blank-wall control too.
- Ethics: harmless; the subtraction stressor is mild.
- Verdict: interlude — a calm, seated, easy-to-build 6-minute scene with a modest, expectation-prone mood effect and a small error-count effect; useful as a palate cleanser between rooms, not as a room with a reveal.
