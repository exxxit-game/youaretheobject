## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: two questionnaire pages plus at most 6 minutes of smoke, so about 8–10 minutes. Fits.
- Task/tension: a boring questionnaire, then smoke that gets worse. The tension depends on the smoke possibly being a real fire. In VR there is no smell, no coughing and no stinging eyes, so only the visual build-up remains.
- Within-person reveal: weak. Each subject was in only one condition (alone, or with others). One player gets one condition. The reveal can show the player's own time to leave next to the paper's numbers (alone: median 2 min, 75% reported; with passive others: 10%). A second run in the other condition is not valid, because the player now knows the trick.
- Works if the player expects tricks: badly. The original needed naive participants, and even they sometimes decided "it must be some sort of experiment" and stayed. In Kinateder & Warren (2016), 68% of VR participants thought a fire alarm was part of the experiment. A player of a psychology game will expect the smoke to be a test, and that pushes even the "alone" player to stay. So the comparison loses meaning.
- Space tier: roomscale. The paper gives no room size ("a small waiting room"). The subject sits, may walk to the vent, and walks out of the door to report.
- VR or mixed reality: VR suits the original better. The exit door and the place to report are part of the scene. Mixed reality would put the smoke in the player's own room, which might raise the sense of danger, but the act of leaving to report then has nowhere to go. Untested.
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy to build: a seated questionnaire, particle smoke from a vent, two scripted NPCs and a door. Time to leave or to report is logged automatically by a door trigger. No eye tracking is needed. The paper times reports from the moment the subject first notices the smoke, a moment that observers judged by eye. Without eye tracking this can only be guessed from head direction.
- Replication: the paper's three conditions are one study at one site. Kinateder & Warren (2016) is a separate VR study that used a fire alarm, not smoke: the passive-bystander effect showed up, but weaker in VR.
- Ethics: deception (cover-story interview, fake smoke, scripted indifferent NPCs). It needs a debrief, which the paper does not describe. VR smoke is harmless, but the scene imitates a fire, so players with fire trauma need a way out. A content warning given beforehand works against keeping the player naive.
- Verdict: room — cheap to build and measured automatically, but the effect needs naive players who believe the danger may be real, and a known psychology game undermines both. Not a first room.
