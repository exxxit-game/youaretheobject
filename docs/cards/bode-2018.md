## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: three short training rounds and a 45-second test: about 3 minutes.
- Task/tension: a real game decision: grab more coins or get out before the timer ends, with the crowd leaving around you. The pressure is the clock and the risk of losing everything.
- Within-person reveal: one test run per person. The reveal can place the player's coin count next to the paper's split (26.5% none, 18.1% all) and show that people told they were recovering "lost" coins took more. That framing effect is between players, so one player sees only their own side of it.
- Works if the player expects tricks: well. It is openly a game with no deception, so a suspicious player does the same task. Only the framing must stay unnoticed.
- Space tier: seated.
- VR or mixed reality: the original is a flat top-down screen. Mixed reality keeps it closest: the floor plan as a tabletop model on the player's real table, steered with the controller ray. A first-person "grab your belongings and run" room in VR would be a new experiment, not this one. It can also run unchanged for desktop players.
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy: a small crowd simulation, a pointer, a timer and a coin counter, all logged automatically.
- Replication: one very large study (1229), not replicated. The effect of other people is weak: the late crowd changed only the share of zero-coin players, not the average. The robust result is the loss framing.
- Ethics: harmless; the original included children from 7 years old. No threat, nothing frightening.
- Verdict: interlude — robust, automatic, harmless and already game-shaped; but the other-people effect it was built to test is weak, and its strong result (loss framing) is a between-player comparison.
