## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: not stated. One anthem with cups, then 5–6 game rounds and a few questions: about 8–12 minutes. That is an estimate.
- Task/tension: singing and moving together is light; the tension is the later money choice (choose 7 and risk the group's weak link, or give tokens and risk free-riders).
- Within-person reveal: weak. Each group is in one condition, so a player cannot compare themselves across conditions (as in reddish-2013). The reveal can show the group's own choices against the paper's curves.
- Works if the player expects tricks: partly. The asynchronous condition relies on a cover ("you may hear different versions"), and the shared payment was not real. A player who guesses the hypothesis may cooperate to please.
- Live players needed: 3 at the same time for the song and all game rounds.
- Comparison with reddish-2013: both find that synchrony raises cooperation and that happiness does not explain it (Reddish: only trust tracked choices; here a "same team" feeling did). Reddish says synchrony works best together with a shared goal, and here every group had one built in: a shared payment and the experimenter calling them a group. So the two papers agree rather than conflict. This paper adds two things useful for a headset room. First, singing in time alone was as good as singing plus moving, so no body tracking is needed. Second, asynchrony was made purely through what each person heard (different tempos), which a headset does natively. The weak points: Study 1 helped only round 1, Study 2 beat the plain control only marginally, and statistics are reported as p-rep, not p-values.
- Space tier: seated (Studies 2–3, at a table). Study 1 was an outdoor walk around campus with no stated distance and has no home version.
- VR or mixed reality: the original is three people at one table seeing and hearing each other, so mixed reality suits a group in one room. Remote players would need VR avatars and transmitted voices, which this paper did not test.
- Quest 3 feasibility: cups become controllers; game choices are logged automatically; the per-player tempo for the asynchronous condition is easy. The catch is the same as reddish-2013: network delay. Each player can be locked to the music by a shared clock, but hears and sees the others late. Singing together over the internet sounds out of sync. Whether singing in time with the music without hearing the others in time still counts is untested. "O Canada" is an out-group anthem only for non-Canadians; for Canadian players it is their own anthem, a different condition. The music is old, but any recording used needs its own rights check.
- Replication: three studies in one paper (30, 96, 105 people) pointing the same way, with small groups-of-3 samples and several marginal results. Independent replications are not checked here; reddish-2013 is a conceptual replication with a different explanation.
- Ethics: two mild deceptions (the "different versions" cover and the performance pay that was really a flat $4). A game would use points and must debrief. Voice: if singing is timed, only onset times are computed on the headset; no audio leaves it.
- Verdict: room (3 live players, pilot first) — short, automatically measured, and it needs only voice or music, not body tracking; but between groups only, built on small samples with marginal results, and dependent on network delay not destroying the felt synchrony.
