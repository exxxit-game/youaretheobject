## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: about 10 minutes for eight questions (8 × 20 s alone, 4 × 60 s in the group, 8 × 20 s again; our arithmetic, not stated).
- Task/tension: social and lively: five strangers must agree on a number in one minute.
- Within-person reveal: yes — each player sees their first guess, the group answer and their revised guess against the true value, and how their group compares with the crowd of all players. The "4 groups beat thousands" effect itself is a statistic across groups.
- Works if the player expects tricks: yes; there is no trick and no deception, only real answers and real arithmetic.
- Space tier: seated (auditorium seats; the lab repeat also seated groups).
- VR or mixed reality: VR suits better: the five players are in different homes, so a shared virtual room with avatars and voice is the closest equivalent to sitting together. Mixed reality would place the others' avatars in one's own room; possible, but it adds nothing the paper needs.
- Quest 3 feasibility: needs five live players at once (Supabase realtime) and voice between them (microphone with permission, unverified on our headset). Desktop players could join the same group. Answers and timing are logged automatically. Matchmaking five people at the same moment is the real cost.
- Replication: the paper includes its own lab replication (N = 100); the plain wisdom-of-crowds effect is old (Galton, cited); whether social influence helps or hurts crowds is disputed in the literature the paper reviews — the benefit here depends on discussion in small independent groups.
- Ethics: voice chat between strangers needs moderation, a mute/leave button and no recording; questions must be neutral.
- Verdict: room — the strongest candidate for the first multi-player room: no deception, short, automatic, and a striking group-level reveal; blocked only by five-player matchmaking and voice.
