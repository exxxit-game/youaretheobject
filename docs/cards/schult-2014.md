## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: 10–15 minutes for a shortened list (the 20-minute Exp. 1 had several study–test cycles); recognition alone is quick.
- Task/tension: "memorise these actions"; one player performs each spoken action in pantomime, the other watches. The tension is the surprise test and the comparison between partners.
- Within-person reveal: yes, and a between-partner one: after the test both players see who recognised more, and the pair can swap roles for a second list so each player experiences both.
- Works if the player expects tricks: yes; there is no deception, only a memory test, though players who know the hypothesis might rehearse more as watchers.
- Space tier: standing (pantomime in place, facing the partner; no walking).
- VR or mixed reality: VR suits a networked pair (each sees the other's avatar perform with tracked hands and head); mixed reality suits two players in the same room, who can see each other's real bodies through passthrough, closest to the original face-to-face pair.
- Quest 3 feasibility: good. Hand tracking shows the actor's pantomime on an avatar for a remote partner; the recognition test (old/new for each phrase) is automatic with two buttons. Free recall needs typing or speech recognition (microphone permission); recognition alone avoids that. The game can check that the actor actually moved during each phrase.
- Replication: the recognition advantage was found twice in this paper (Exp. 1B and 2); the free-recall null between performers and watchers is, by the authors' account, typical of between-subject designs, while the wider enactment literature (verbal-learning controls) usually finds a recall advantage. Mixed, honestly reported.
- Ethics: harmless; the "confederate" is only a fill-in partner.
- Verdict: room (two players) — a natural use of live players; with a networked partner it answers a question the original could not: does watching an avatar's tracked hands count as observing a person?
