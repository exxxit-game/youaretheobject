## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: the paper's 60 minutes covers instructions, the payoff test, two 6-period games and questionnaires. One 6-period punishment game with a short timer per decision would take roughly 8–12 minutes. That is an estimate, not the paper's protocol.
- Task/tension: the same real tension as herrmann-2008: give or free-ride, then pay to hurt others. What is added here is that you will never meet the person you punish again, so punishing cannot pay you back.
- Within-person reveal: good. The reveal can show whom the player punished (below- or above-average givers), how much it cost them, and how their own giving changed after being punished. These can be set next to the paper's 84.3%, 74.2% and +1.62 MU. With both games, the player also sees their own jump when punishment is switched on or off.
- Works if the player expects tricks: yes. There is no deception and the rules are told in full. Knowing the study may make players nicer; the paper cannot say how much.
- Live players needed: 24 at once in the original, and at least 19 for the "never meet anyone twice" rule. In 6 periods in groups of four, each player meets 3 × 6 = 18 different others. Four fixed players is not this experiment. It is the partner design of herrmann-2008 (fixed group, 10 periods), where punishing can pay off later, so it is not "altruistic" in this paper's sense.
- Comparison with herrmann-2008: Herrmann reused this game (20 tokens, 0.4 return, punishment points costing 1 and taking 3) but with fixed groups and 10 periods, in 16 pools that include Zurich and St. Gallen. Both papers find that punishment raises giving in the Swiss pools (in Herrmann's figures, Zurich averaged 9.3 tokens without punishment and 16.2 with it; St. Gallen 10.1 and 16.7). Fehr & Gächter mostly describe punishment of low givers. Herrmann's main finding, punishment of high givers ("antisocial"), appears here only as a regression term and as the 25.8% of punishments that were not "cooperator punishes defector". A reveal built on this paper alone would therefore understate antisocial punishment outside Western pools.
- Space tier: seated.
- VR or mixed reality: neither suits it better. The original is a screen in a booth, so a flat panel in VR or a desktop is closest. Anonymity is central: avatars must not identify players, and voice must be off.
- Quest 3 feasibility: the interface is easy (number entry with controllers, all choices logged automatically). The blocker is 19–24 simultaneous players with re-matching each period. Points replace the paper's real money, which may change both giving and punishing. The hypothetical anger questions could be asked of a single player, but that is self-report only.
- Replication: inside the paper, 10 sessions, both orders, and an outside group that answered the emotion scenarios the same way. herrmann-2008 extends the game to 16 pools with fixed groups and confirms the effect of punishment in the Swiss pools. Independent stranger-matching replications are not checked here.
- Ethics: no deception. Being punished by strangers can upset players, so a short debrief is needed. Points instead of money.
- Verdict: room (multiplayer event, at least 19 and ideally 24 live players) — the real test of altruistic punishment, deception-free and fully automatic, but the stranger design needs a large simultaneous pool. Build herrmann-2008's 4-player version first and keep this as a scheduled event.
