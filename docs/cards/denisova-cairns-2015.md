## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: the paper used 20-minute rounds (about 45 min in all); an Object version would need two short rounds (3–5 min each), which is untested.
- Task/tension: good — the player plays a real mini-game twice and is not told what is being studied.
- Within-person reveal: strong in Study I form — "both rounds were identical random worlds; you rated the 'AI' round higher and described what the AI did"; the player's own words make it vivid.
- Works if the player expects tricks: poorly — the paper itself shows experts were less fooled; players of a psychology game will suspect a fake feature.
- Space tier: seated (desktop game).
- VR or mixed reality: neither matters; any short VR mini-game with a random level generator works. VR has no advantage over the original desktop game.
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy — two rounds of the same random-level mini-game, a short in-headset immersion questionnaire after each, and an optional free-text or spoken "what did the AI do?".
- Replication: Study II in the same paper replicated it between groups; Denisova & Cairns (2019, Entertainment Computing; abstract only) report the same for claimed difficulty adaptation; Kosch et al. (2022, ACM TOCHI, arXiv full text read in scratch; N = 369 and 100) found raised expectations from a sham adaptive AI but no change in error rate — so self-reports move, objective performance may not.
- Ethics: mild deception about a feature, always followed by a reveal; no pain or risk.
- Verdict: room — no pain, seated, automatic ratings and a personal "the AI was never there" reveal; it must be piloted with short rounds and is weak on suspicious players, and only ratings (not performance) can be promised to change.
