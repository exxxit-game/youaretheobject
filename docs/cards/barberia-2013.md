## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: the lesson is about 80 min (far beyond 5–15 min); the measuring task alone (2 × 40 patients, one choice and one look each) is likely 5–10 min, but the paper does not state it.
- Task/tension: low — click "give / don't give" 40 times and watch the outcome; the tension is only in the final rating.
- Within-person reveal: the paper's finding (the lesson lowers the illusion) is visible only between groups — one player cannot see it in themselves. What one player can see: their own rating of a medicine that did nothing, next to their own table (recovered with it vs without it, both 75%). That reveal is the classic causal-illusion task, not this paper's contribution.
- Works if the player expects tricks: doubtful. The paper's own lesson group had just been deceived and rated the useless medicine much lower; a player who expects tricks is in a similar state, so the illusion may shrink.
- Space tier: seated (classroom computer task; the staged exercises are part of the lesson, not of the measure).
- Quest 3 feasibility: the measuring task is easy (two buttons, automatic log of choices and the 0–100 rating). The 80-min lesson with a group discussion and a fake product handed out by an experimenter cannot be done by one player at home.
- Replication: none in the paper (it calls itself the first such study).
- Ethics: deception with a fake product followed by a reveal; fine with a clear debrief.
- Verdict: interlude — the 40-patient medicine task fits a short seated scene with a personal table reveal, but the paper's actual finding (the lesson works) is between groups and needs 80 minutes.
