## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: the lab session was 3 hours (110 turns in E1 alone). A turning-only block with fewer turns (for example a 20–30-turn staircase) would take about 5–8 min; that shortening is an estimate and untested.
- Task/tension: a dry judgement ("was the world's turn bigger or smaller than yours?"); little tension by itself.
- Within-person reveal: yes for turning — the headset knows the real turn, so the game can show "you turned 125°, you saw 90°".
- Works if the player expects tricks: yes — subjects knew they were manipulated and their job was to catch it, so the thresholds are already measured under suspicion.
- Space tier: standing for E1 (turning on the spot); large 10 × 7 m for E2 and E3 (real walks of up to 7 m in a 10 × 7 m lab). E2–E3 are offered only to players with that much space, never shrunk.
- Quest 3 feasibility: E1 needs only head rotation and one button — easy on Quest 3 and wireless (no cable to wrap). Quest 3's field of view is much wider than the 40° headset used here, which gives more visual cues and may lower the thresholds; untested. No eye tracking or force feedback needed.
- Replication: only the authors' own earlier study [31]; 12 analysed subjects per experiment, 2 of them authors.
- Ethics: simulator sickness rose over the session (8.55 → 24.04); turning on the spot needs a clear stationary boundary; otherwise none special.
- Verdict: interlude — a short standing turning test (E1) gives a personal number with an honest reveal, but the task is a dry judgement with little tension; E2–E3 only for players with about 10 × 7 m.
