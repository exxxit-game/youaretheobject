## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: about 4–6 minutes (one practice, five trials with 30 s delays).
- Task/tension: a memory chore with an ambiguous instruction; the interest is that there are two correct answers and most people never notice.
- Within-person reveal: medium. The game can show the player that their rebuilt row matched their body (left/right), not the room (north/south), that in the bare room only 20% asked during the task which was meant, and that Tzeltal speakers do the opposite. The landmark manipulation in the paper is between groups; giving one player both duck placements would be a change (and once the ambiguity is noticed it stays noticed).
- Works if the player expects tricks: partly. A suspicious player is more likely to notice the ambiguity, which is exactly what landmarks did in the paper (70% asked); the game could record that as data, but it changes the measure.
- Space tier: seated (swivel chair turned 180°; the original Brown & Levinson version walked between tables).
- VR or mixed reality: both make sense and differ. VR can reproduce the featureless room and the duck landmark exactly. Mixed reality puts the tables in the player's own furnished room, which is the paper's "landmarks visible" case, so MR would be expected to raise absolute answers; that comparison is not in the paper.
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy and automatic. Grabbing and placing three animals with controllers or hands, the 180° turn is tracked by head yaw, and the left/right vs compass-direction choice is read directly from the placed order. Turning in a seated or standing position needs a swivel chair or a cleared spot.
- Replication: contested. This paper reproduced the Dutch-like default in English speakers, then showed landmarks could flip it; Levinson, Kita, Haun & Rasch (2002, "Returning the tables", Cognition 84) replied that the duck condition changes the task and that the language effect stands (rebuttal not read here). The default "relative" answer of English/Dutch speakers in a bare room is the stable part.
- Ethics: none special; the reveal should not tell players their language "limits" their thinking, since this paper argues it does not.
- Verdict: interlude: a short, automatic, body-in-space task with a genuine "two right answers" reveal; the headline cross-language contrast cannot be shown by one player and the landmark effect is between groups and disputed.
