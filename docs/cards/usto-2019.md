## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: 18 judgements, probably 5–10 minutes (not stated).
- Task/tension: real social pressure: five people calmly give an obviously wrong answer before you.
- Within-person reveal: yes — "you followed the wrong majority in N of 12 critical trials" is personal; the similarity effect is group-level ("you vs others").
- Works if the player expects tricks: weak. The paper excluded people who knew the Asch paradigm; most players of a psychology game will know it. Expect lower conformity than 35%.
- Space tier: seated (participants sat in a row, the cards 3 m away).
- VR or mixed reality: VR suits better: the majority must be virtual people in a controlled lab room. Mixed reality would put the five avatars in the player's own room, which changes the social setting and adds nothing the paper needs.
- Quest 3 feasibility: possible with scripted avatars (no live confederates at home); their voices, timing and believability decide the result. With several live players the "confederates" cannot be other players without deceiving them; the live-player route is the Mori & Arai (2010) split-display method, cited in this paper (papers folder has mori-arai-2010.txt, no card yet).
- Replication: the Asch effect is replicated in many countries (the paper's own context) but also failed in some (Perrin & Spencer 1981); this study adds a collectivist-leaning sample (its own interpretation) and an in-group/out-group moderation.
- Ethics: the ethnic in-group/out-group manipulation is not acceptable for a game; a neutral group cue (team colour, "players from your city") would be a different manipulation and needs its own evidence. Deception about the majority needs a debrief.
- Verdict: room — Asch with scripted avatars; use the Bosnian numbers as one reference point, do not reproduce the ethnic manipulation.
