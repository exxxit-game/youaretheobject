## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: not stated in the paper; Segall's 12 items or Rivers' 10 adjustment trials take a few minutes, so a room with several figure variants fits 5–10 min.
- Task/tension: simple and immediate — "make the two lines equal" with the controller (Rivers' sliding box is a ready VR mechanic); the tension comes when the player is shown the true lengths and still sees them as unequal.
- Within-person reveal: strong — the player's own setting is compared with the true length, then the fins are removed or the lines measured in front of them, and the illusion stays.
- Works if the player expects tricks: largely yes — the paper's core point is that the illusion survives knowing about it. Risk: in an adjustment task a knowing player can deliberately "correct" for it; a quick forced-choice staircase (which line is longer, short exposure) is harder to game than free adjustment.
- Space tier: seated (both described procedures are tabletop viewing or adjusting; no movement).
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy — lines rendered on a virtual board at a fixed distance, thumbstick or hand adjustment, automatic logging of the point of subjective equality per trial. Check thin-line rendering at the headset's resolution and keep the board world-locked. VR also allows something paper cannot: fins drawn as real 3D convex/concave corners, a direct test of Gregory's "corners" explanation described on pp. 4 and 13.
- Replication: the illusion itself is among the most replicated effects (the paper calls it the subject of thousands of studies); its cultural variation is what the paper disputes. The card's paper is a review: no protocol numbers come from it, so the room's protocol must be taken from a primary study read separately (e.g. Gandhi et al. 2015, or a modern psychophysics paper).
- What it would take to call it science: a pre-registered psychophysics protocol (forced-choice staircase or adjustment with randomised start lengths, both fin orientations, counterbalanced order), a fixed virtual viewing distance, a priori exclusions, and a clear question — e.g. does illusion size differ within a player between arrow fins, semicircles, dots and real 3D corners. A cultural claim cannot be tested on Quest owners: almost all grew up in carpentered settings, so childhood environment would be a weak, self-reported covariate only.
- Ethics: none special.
- Verdict: first-room candidate — robust, seated, measured automatically in minutes, with a reveal that survives the player knowing the trick; condition: build the protocol from a primary paper, since this one is a review.
