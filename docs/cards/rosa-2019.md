## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: one condition is 2 minutes; a synchronous-vs-asynchronous pair is about 5 minutes plus questions. The original six conditions took about 36 min.
- Task/tension: none. The player sits still, watches and taps a finger. The knife threat is the only dramatic moment.
- Within-person reveal: only through questionnaires. The objective measure (skin conductance) showed no condition effect, so there is nothing automatic to show back.
- Works if the player expects tricks: unclear; the measure is a self-report, which is sensitive to expectations. The authors note that willingness to accept a virtual limb matters.
- Space tier: seated.
- VR or mixed reality: mixed reality is the original. The point is a virtual hand added while the real hands stay visible, which only exists with passthrough. Quest 3 passthrough shows the real hands as the original did.
- Quest 3 feasibility: poor. The working extra hand was a live depth-camera copy of the player's own hand. Quest Browser WebXR gives no camera image, so only a generic model hand is possible, and that version failed in the pilot (ownership negative). Finger-tap mirroring needs hand tracking, which is unverified on our headset. Wrist vibration could only come from a held controller, and that blocks the bare hand.
- Replication: the paper reports one main study plus a pilot. The authors' own discussion says the response levels resemble earlier rubber-hand studies in reality (Guterstam et al.). A follow-up by the same group exists (Frontiers in Computer Science 2021), not read here.
- Ethics: a virtual knife threat, announced beforehand in the original. Some discomfort was reported; no lasting effects.
- Verdict: reject: it was measured only by questionnaire (skin conductance showed nothing), and it worked only with a live video copy of the player's own hand. Quest 3 WebXR cannot show that, and the model-hand version failed in the pilot.
