## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: Exp. 1 took 1.5 hours with 7 films; a single-film version (Exp. 2) is a few minutes, but its key measure comes one week later.
- Task/tension: low — watch a crash, answer a questionnaire.
- Within-person reveal: no for the main effect — each person gets one verb, so the speed shift is visible only between groups; one player cannot see it in themselves. The broken-glass "yes" is a personal false memory that could be revealed, but in the paper it needs a one-week delay and only about 1 in 3 "smashed" players say yes (control 6 of 50, Table 2).
- Works if the player expects tricks: poor — a leading question in a psychology game is easy to spot, and this is one of the best-known textbook studies.
- Space tier: seated (watching films, paper questionnaire).
- Quest 3 feasibility: showing a crash and logging a speed estimate is easy; the one-week retest needs a second session, and the between-group comparison needs other players' data.
- Replication: none in the paper (two experiments by the same authors).
- Ethics: mild — car-crash footage; no deception beyond the wording.
- Verdict: reject — the speed effect exists only between groups, the false-glass memory needs a week and appears in about a third of players, and the study is too famous to surprise.
