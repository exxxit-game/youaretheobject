## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: under a minute — far too short for a room; fits only as a moment between rooms.
- Task/tension: low. A stranger asks a big favour, then a smaller one. In the original, "yes" meant real future hours of volunteering; in a game, saying yes to a character costs nothing unless the request takes real playing time or effort from the player (e.g. "help me for five more minutes").
- Within-person reveal: no. The effect is a difference in percentages between groups (51% vs 34%), and each person answers once; one player's yes or no says nothing about whether the technique worked on them. The only reveal is "here is what 51% of people did".
- Works if the player expects tricks: badly. Door-in-the-face is a textbook persuasion trick, and a psychology-game player expects to be manipulated, so the big request is likely to be seen through.
- Space tier: standing (a short face-to-face conversation; the original passerby was stopped on campus).
- Quest 3 feasibility: technically trivial — one character, voice or button answer, logged automatically. The hard part is making the request real.
- Replication: strong — a preregistered direct replication with 391 people and 30 experimenters reproduced the original almost exactly (51.3% vs 54.1%); experimenters were not blind to condition.
- Ethics: the original used a made-up counselling programme and debriefed people; in a game the request must be honoured or explained in the reveal.
- Verdict: interlude — one of the best-replicated findings on this list, but a sub-minute, between-groups, one-answer effect with no personal reveal, and only meaningful if the small request costs the player something real.
