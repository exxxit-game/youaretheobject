## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: 8 trials of up to 2 minutes, plus a short maze training, so about 8–18 minutes. Trimmed training keeps it inside 15.
- Task/tension: get out of a smoke-filled tunnel. Each trial the same virtual man either runs to the exit, runs away, or just stands there. The tension is real (state anxiety rose).
- Within-person reveal: yes, partly. Every player meets all four conditions twice, and the paper plotted each participant's paths. A replay from above of the player's own eight paths can show "you walked over to the man who stood still before going on". That detour was seen in most participants. The exit/no-exit split is a weaker personal reveal: only 2 trials per condition, and many participants went to the exit every time.
- Works if the player expects tricks: better than most social rooms. Participants knew it was a tunnel-safety experiment and saw the man behave differently trial after trial, and the effects still appeared. A player in a psychology game may deliberately ignore the man. That threat is real but smaller than in single-trial deception designs (compare kinateder-warren-2016).
- Space tier: standing (the participant stood in place 2 m from a projection wall and moved through the tunnel with a joystick; no real walking).
- VR or mixed reality: VR. The original is itself a VR study, and a smoky tunnel cannot be put into the player's room. The headset is a change from the original projection wall: wider view, head-tracked, more immersive. Thumbstick movement matches the original gamepad joystick.
- Quest 3 feasibility: straightforward. Build a tunnel, layered fog, one animated man and thumbstick locomotion. Destination, pre-movement time, movement time and path length are all logged automatically, as in the paper. Risks: smooth thumbstick movement at a fixed 2.86 m/s in a headset may cause simulator sickness (the original used a projection wall); dense volumetric smoke may be heavy for the Quest browser; the fixed trial order and the exit-side confound should be fully randomised, as the authors themselves recommend.
- Replication: one study, 40 people, one lab, within subjects with an order confound. The same group's earlier VR study found no passive effect. Kinateder & Warren (2016) found the passive-bystander effect weaker in VR. The direction (others' behaviour pulls you) is consistent with Latané & Darley.
- Ethics: no deception. Fire and smoke raise anxiety, so warn players with tunnel or fire anxiety and give an exit at any time. The original debriefed everyone.
- Verdict: room — VR-native, standing, deception-free, automatically measured, with a personal path replay; but it rests on one 40-person study with an order confound and an earlier null from the same lab, and running-speed thumbstick movement in a headset needs a comfort pilot.
