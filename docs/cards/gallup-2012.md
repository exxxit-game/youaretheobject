## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: a walk-past lasts seconds; one trial is 60 s. Far too short for a room; fits as a moment inside another walk.
- Task/tension: none for the player — it is incidental. The player has their own errand; a group nearby stares up at nothing.
- Within-person reveal: yes, as a single moment — whether and how long the player's head pitched up when the group looked up. The paper itself measured head direction, not eyes, so Quest's head tracking measures the same thing with no eye tracking. A within-person dose-response (1 vs 15 starers) would need repeated passes, which breaks the naive-passerby design.
- Works if the player expects tricks: badly. The effect depends on passersby who do not know they are in an experiment and are busy walking; a player in a psychology game scans everything and will look up at any staring group, or deliberately not.
- Space tier: large 10 × 8 m (Exp. 1: passersby walked through the filmed street section). Exp. 2's station hall had mostly stationary people with the effect inside 2 m — that variant would be standing, but it tests suspicious actors, not the look-up crowd.
- Quest 3 feasibility: technically simple (NPC crowd, head-pitch logging). The faithful walk-through is offered only to players with about a 10 × 8 m boundary; thumbstick or teleport walking would be a different experiment.
- Replication: the paper partly replicates Milgram et al. (1969) with a weaker response (max 66% vs 92%); 2,822 people in Exp. 1, a large field sample.
- Ethics: none special for a consenting player; the original passersby were unaware and anonymous.
- Verdict: interlude (large space only) — a few-second incidental moment with an automatic head-pitch measure, too short for a room and spoiled if the player is looking for tricks.
