## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: the original first stage took 35 min (15 min in the low-effort version); the allocation itself takes a few minutes. A game version is perhaps 8–12 min.
- Task/tension: split money between "your team" and "the other team" while knowing (or not) that your own pay depends on what others give you.
- Within-person reveal: weak — one decision per person; the finding is a difference between groups of players ("those who could be paid back gave their team more").
- Works if the player expects tricks: poorly; the dot-counting group label is a known cover, and players who know the minimal-group studies may act fair on purpose.
- Space tier: seated.
- VR or mixed reality: neither; the original keeps people anonymous in booths with no face-to-face contact, so avatars would change the "minimal" condition. Desktop players can take it too.
- Quest 3 feasibility: trivial technically; the real requirement is several live players at once (or asynchronous matching) so that the two-way dependence is real rather than staged.
- Replication: the paper reports agreement with Karp et al. (1993) and Jin (1992); the Exp. 2 link to the "illusion of control" is correlational by the authors' own account.
- Ethics: the original used deception (everyone in the same group; everyone "chosen" as allocator; confederates) and real money; with live players the dependence can be real, but points instead of money and honest group assignment would be needed.
- Verdict: interlude — a multiplayer decision with a between-player result; no body, space or headset needed.
