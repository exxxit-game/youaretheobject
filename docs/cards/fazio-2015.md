## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: the original is 264 ratings plus a knowledge check (duration not stated; probably 30–45 minutes). A game version would need far fewer statements (for example 20 exposed, 40 tested), which shrinks the per-player difference.
- Task/tension: none; reading and rating sentences. The surprise comes only at the reveal.
- Within-person reveal: real but modest: "you rated repeated statements truer than new ones, including false ones you could answer correctly". The average shift is about a quarter to a third of a scale point (0.2–0.34 on the 6-point scale), so with few items some players will show nothing.
- Works if the player expects tricks: partly; the paper already warned participants that some statements were repeated and the effect still appeared. A player who knows the illusory truth effect may correct deliberately.
- Space tier: seated.
- VR or mixed reality: neither; the original is text on a screen, and long reading in a headset is tiring. This fits the desktop version of the game better than the headset.
- Quest 3 feasibility: technically trivial, but text-heavy and language-dependent: the statements are US trivia norms and must be re-normed per language and culture (what is "known" differs), which is a large job.
- Replication: one of the most replicated effects in memory and persuasion research (the paper says dozens of replications); the "knowledge does not protect" part comes from this paper's two experiments.
- Ethics: players are shown false statements; every falsehood must be corrected at the end, and none should touch health or politics.
- Verdict: interlude (desktop rather than headset): robust and within-person, the core mechanism of misinformation, but it uses neither the body nor space and needs re-normed statements in every game language.
