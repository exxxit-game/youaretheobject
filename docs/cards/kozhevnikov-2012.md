## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: 72 trials at about 5.4 s each, about 7–8 min plus practice; fits as one block.
- Task/tension: a puzzle with a clock (same or mirror?); mild, not dramatic.
- Within-person reveal: good and honest — the game plots the player's own response time against angle and shows a near-straight line ("your mind turned the shape at about N° per second"), plus the axis difference.
- Works if the player expects tricks: yes; there is no trick, and knowing about mental rotation does not remove the time cost.
- Space tier: seated.
- VR or mixed reality: VR suits the original (fully enclosed scene; Exp. 2 says the viewer-centred pattern needs enclosure). Mixed reality is a genuine open test: figures floating in the player's real room might restore the desktop-like, scene-based pattern.
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy — two block figures, two controller buttons, response time logged per trial (90 Hz frame timing is precise enough for 2–10 s responses).
- Replication: the linear RT–angle function (Shepard & Metzler 1971) is among the most replicated effects in cognitive psychology, and it also appears in recent HMD versions (e.g., a 2023 Frontiers in Neuroscience eye-tracking VR study, abstract only). The paper's specific immersive finding (depth slower than picture plane) rests on 12 people in an interaction effect; no independent replication was found.
- Ethics: none special.
- Verdict: interlude — reliable, seated, automatic, with a clean personal line-plot reveal, but it replays a textbook effect with a dry task; the immersion-specific twist is too weak (n = 12, unreplicated) to present as a finding.
