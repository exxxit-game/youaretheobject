## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: 1–2 minutes (20 quick judgements and one critical comparison). An interlude, not a room.
- Task/tension: none during the task (a dull "which figure is higher" game); the surprise is the single critical screen and the reveal.
- Within-person reveal: yes, but thin — one critical answer per player ("you said the left had more; both had 100, and for 20 screens the left had fewer"). The size of the effect is a group statistic.
- Works if the player expects tricks: uncertain for this dot version — a suspicious player may simply answer "equal". The cited anecdote is about the haptic ball version, where knowing did not remove the feeling.
- Space tier: seated (laptop screen).
- VR or mixed reality: either; the original is a small screen. A panel in VR, or a "screen" on the player's real desk in mixed reality, are equally close. The classic hand version (balls in both hands) cannot be built: Quest controllers are the same size in both hands and there is no force feedback.
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy and fully automatic (desktop players too). The critical screen must appear without warning.
- Replication: Uznadze's set illusion is old and repeatedly reported (this paper reviews his tables and Grigolava's work); this particular "unattended dots" version is a single study of 50 people with one critical trial each and no control group without a setting series, and no significance test is reported in the text. Part of the 84% "error" may be guessing on a hard 100-vs-100 comparison; the contrast-versus-assimilation split (29 vs 13) is the real evidence.
- Ethics: a mild cover story (reaction-speed test); debrief.
- Verdict: interlude — a one-minute between-room moment with an automatic answer; to count as evidence the game must add the missing control group (critical screen without the setting series) and report the result across players.
