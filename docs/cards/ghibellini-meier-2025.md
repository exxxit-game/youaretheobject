## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: one interrupted task plus a free waiting period fits 5–10 min.
- Task/tension: mild — an engaging puzzle cut off mid-way, then a pause where nobody asks the player to continue.
- Within-person reveal: yes for resumption — the game logs whether the player went back to the unfinished puzzle without being asked and can show it. It is a single yes/no, and about a third of people do not resume, so for them the reveal is weaker. The Zeigarnik memory effect should not be used: the meta-analysis finds none on average.
- Works if the player expects tricks: doubtful — in a game, going back to an unfinished puzzle is normal player behaviour, and a suspicious player may deliberately leave it; the paper also reports that a monetary incentive lowered resumption, so motivation framing matters.
- Space tier: seated (desk puzzles and computer tasks in the included studies).
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy — a puzzle on a table, a staged interruption (fake "loading" or "network" message), then free time with the puzzle still there and an alternative object; resumption is detected automatically.
- Replication: the meta-analysis itself is the replication record — resumption holds across about 20 publications; recall advantage does not.
- Ethics: a staged interruption is a mild deception; debrief after.
- Verdict: interlude — Ovsiankina resumption gives a short seated scene with an automatic yes/no and a personal reveal; Zeigarnik recall is not reliable and should not be built.
