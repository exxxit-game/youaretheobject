## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: one problem takes a few minutes (dropped after 2–3 minutes without new ideas). Duncker's full set of five problems would take about 10–20 minutes.
- Task/tension: a practical puzzle at a cluttered table, with a clear "aha" when the box becomes a shelf.
- Within-person reveal: weak for the box problem alone. Each person met the box only in one setting (full or empty), so 3 of 7 against 7 of 7 is a between-groups result. A player who fails can still have the "scales fall from their eyes" moment when shown the box. Duncker's five-problem design is better: each person alternates with and without pre-use across the five problems, so one player can see "the objects you had just used for something else took you longer".
- Works if the player expects tricks: badly. The candle-and-box solution is very widely known, and a player who knows it solves it at once. A puzzle-game player in escape-room mode tries every object for every use, which may remove the fixedness the original relied on. Asking "have you seen this before?" is unreliable (chandler-2015). The other four problems (gimlet, pliers, weight, paperclip) are much less known.
- Space tier: standing (a table of objects next to a door at eye height; the paper gives no room sizes).
- VR or mixed reality: mixed reality is closer to the original. Duncker used a real door and a real table, and mixed reality can put the virtual board on the player's real wall at eye height and the objects on the real table. VR is easier to control (identical clutter, lighting and box placement for every player). Either way, picking up tacks, emptying boxes and pinning a box to a wall needs reliable two-handed grabbing with hands or controllers.
- Quest 3 feasibility: solved or not, time to solution and which objects were picked up can all be logged automatically. Duncker counted a briefly touched object as a quarter pre-solution in the signal experiment, though not in the main one; either rule maps onto grab events. Spoken proposals (think-aloud) need the microphone and speech recognition, so they are only partly automatic. The hard part is object physics: tacks through cardboard into a wall, candles standing on a box.
- Replication: within this chapter, 7 people per setting for the box problem; the signal experiment is admitted inconclusive; the neutral-contents result went against Duncker's own prediction. The later incentive version (glucksberg-1962) still has no full text on file. This card cannot judge later replications.
- Ethics: none. The cover ("for visual experiments") is mild, so a short debrief is enough.
- Verdict: interlude — a short, automatically logged "aha" puzzle with a game-wide full-vs-empty statistic; but the effect is between groups on 7 + 7 people and the solution is famous. Duncker's five-problem alternating set is the version that could grow into a room after a pilot.
