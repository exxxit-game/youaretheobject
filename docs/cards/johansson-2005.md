## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: duration not stated; 15 choices of 2–5 s plus six short explanations, likely 5–10 min.
- Task/tension: "which face is more attractive, and why?" — a light task; the tension arrives with the reveal.
- Within-person reveal: yes, very strong — show the face the player chose next to the one they held, and replay their own spoken reasons for the face they did not choose.
- Works if the player expects tricks: poorly — the original relied on a cover story, and the authors themselves expect most people to spot the switch when told to look; a psychology-trick game is close to that framing.
- Space tier: seated (cards on a table, experimenter opposite).
- Quest 3 feasibility: the original was live sleight of hand, but the switch itself is trivial in VR (the card is face down about 2 s), and the authors' own on-screen pilots (19% and 32% detected) show it does not need a live magician. Hard parts: concurrent detection was judged from spontaneous speech, and the reasons are spoken, so the room needs voice capture and speech-to-text or human coding; a "this isn't mine" button would cue detection. Sorting the chosen pictures afterwards can be automated. The face photos come from a research database and would need licensing or other stimuli (the pattern pilot also worked).
- Replication: main study (120) plus three smaller pilots by the same group in this text; no independent replication here.
- Ethics: deception plus asking people to explain choices they did not make; the original debriefed everyone. Voice recording needs consent; rating the attractiveness of real women's faces may be uncomfortable, so neutral stimuli are better.
- Verdict: room — short, seated, with the strongest kind of own-words reveal, but the cover story is part of the method, trick-aware players are the authors' own stated failure case, and the speech-based measures need work.
