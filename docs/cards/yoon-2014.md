## Fit for Object (reviewer's judgment, not facts)
- Participant time: not stated; 12 cards × up to three stages, likely 5 min.
- Task/tension: a small puzzle — "find the eye" in a black-and-white blob, then the photo makes it snap into place.
- Within-person reveal: yes for the familiar "aha" — a player who could not find the animal sees it at once after the photo and cannot unsee it. But in this paper US adults were at ceiling (100%), so the player only rediscovers a well-known effect; the paper's actual finding (Pirahã adults mostly did not reorganize) is a group difference between 9 and 8 people that a player cannot see in themselves.
- Works if the player expects tricks: yes — knowing about the trick does not stop the snap; but puzzle-minded players may find more animals uncued, leaving fewer "aha" trials (US adults already found 72.5% uncued here), so the images must be hard.
- Space tier: seated (cards viewed at arm's length; no movement).
- Quest 3 feasibility: easy — virtual cards held in the hand, pointing with a controller or finger, hit or miss on the eye region scored automatically. The paper's images are CC BY (Figures S1–S12) and could be used with credit.
- Replication: only inside the paper (misaligned-card control, US pilot with 40 images); tiny samples; no independent replication reported.
- Ethics: present the Pirahã as having different visual experience (the paper's "perceptual literacy" idea), not as lacking something.
- Verdict: interlude — a short, seated, automatically scored "aha" moment that survives a suspicious player, but for most players it only replays a known effect at ceiling, and the cultural result is a small group difference no single player can see.
