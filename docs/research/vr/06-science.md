## 1. VR as a research platform (methods reviews)

- **VR's value for psychology = control + reproducibility + ecological validity, but generalisation to real life is untested; participants still know they are in an experiment** — Pan & Hamilton (2018), Why and how to use virtual reality to study human social interaction: The challenges of exploring a new research landscape, *British Journal of Psychology* 109(3), 395–417, doi:10.1111/bjop.12290 — verified yes, full text yes — "no participants are confused between the two" — For us: the reveal must say "in VR you did X", never "in life you would do X".
- **VR studies suit one or two critical events, not hundreds of trials; presence is hard to keep long** — Pan & Hamilton (2018), same — verified yes, full text yes — "participants might experience just one or two critical events" — For us: one-shot rooms are the native VR design; long trial blocks (room 01: 16–20 min) fight the medium.
- **Simulator sickness causes drop-out: 3 of 24 (12.5%) quit a VR maze; a cited 1,102-person study had 6.3% drop-out at 15 min and 45.8% at 60 min (Stanney et al. 2003, cited, not read)** — Pan & Hamilton (2018), same — verified yes (numbers as reported by Pan & Hamilton), full text yes — "three of 24 participants, or 12.5% terminated the task" — For us: keep rooms short, log drop-out per condition, never treat drop-out as random.
- **Ethics in VR: give full information beforehand, stress the right and the way to withdraw, and debrief people who may leave feeling they behaved badly** — Pan & Hamilton (2018), same — verified yes, full text yes — "close your eyes and say STOP to leave the virtual world" — For us: our consent line "снимите шлем или закройте страницу" matches; the reveal must not leave a player feeling like a "bad person".
- **VR data (gaze, motion capture, conversation) is more sensitive than ordinary data** — Pan & Hamilton (2018), same — verified yes, full text yes — "These sensitive and personal information must be dealt with caution" — For us: never send motion or voice traces under the "anonymous" label.
- **Presence = place illusion (being there) + plausibility illusion (events feel real); plausibility needs events that refer to the participant and respond to them** — Slater & Sanchez-Vives (2016), Enhancing our lives with immersive virtual reality, *Frontiers in Robotics and AI* 3, 74, doi:10.3389/frobt.2016.00074 — verified yes, full text partly (sections 1, 2.1, 4, 5, 8 read; sport, travel, journalism skipped) — "Psi is much harder to attain" — For us: virtual agents must react to the player, or plausibility (and the effect) breaks.
- **VR responses are real but weaker because people know it is not real; participants coped by reminding themselves** — Slater & Sanchez-Vives (2016), same (section 4.3, on Slater et al. 2006) — verified yes, full text partly — "Since I kept reminding myself that it wasn't real" — For us: debrief and probe must ask whether the player thought "it's only a game"; it moderates effects.
- **VR moral dilemmas yield more utilitarian action than the same dilemma as text (say/do gap)** — Slater & Sanchez-Vives (2016), same (section 5.1) — verified yes, full text partly — "people become more utilitarian in VR compared to what they will say" — For us: a VR room is a different measure from the paper original; compare like with like.
- **Many VR papers do not even report the equipment; report it** — Slater & Sanchez-Vives (2016), same (section 8.2) — verified yes, full text partly — "many that do not even say what type of equipment was being used" — For us: log headset model, browser version, frame rate, seated/standing with every run (we already plan this).
- **VR distorts space: people underestimate room size and distances; VR side effects are usually mild and adapt with exposure** — Wilson & Soranzo (2015), The use of virtual reality in psychology: A case study in visual perception, *Computational and Mathematical Methods in Medicine* 2015, 151702, doi:10.1155/2015/151702 — verified yes, full text yes — "users consistently underestimate the size of the environment and distance to objects" — For us: rooms whose measure is distance or size must say the VR baseline is already biased.
- **Psychometric validity of VR tasks must be shown; VR adds value only where a paper test cannot answer the question** — Parsons (2015), Virtual reality for enhanced ecological validity and experimental control in the clinical, affective and social neurosciences, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 9, 660, doi:10.3389/fnhum.2015.00660 — verified yes, full text partly (introduction, VR sections, moral dilemmas, summary) — "results reveal the need for judicious use of these virtual platforms" — For us: choose experiments where the body/space is the point (already a project rule).

## 2. Replications of classic experiments in VR, and what changed

- **Virtual Milgram (Cave, not HMD, n = 34): visible learner 17 of 23 gave all 20 shocks, 6 stopped early; hidden learner 11 of 11 gave all; stress shown in skin conductance and heart rate; no deception (everyone knew the learner was virtual)** — Slater et al. (2006), A virtual reprise of the Stanley Milgram obedience experiments, *PLoS ONE* 1(1), e39, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0000039 — verified yes, full text yes — "withdrawal is extremely rare" — For us: VR can study "extreme" social situations without deceiving about harm; numbers are not Milgram's numbers.
- **Prior knowledge was measured and handled: 5 of the 12 who wanted to stop knew Milgram's study; one participant who knew it and pre-decided to quit was excluded** — Slater et al. (2006), same — verified yes, full text yes — "5 claimed to be well-acquainted with the original Milgram study" — For us: famous experiments (Milgram, Asch, bystander) need a "did you know this experiment?" item and analysis by it.
- **In-VR restoration after a distressing scene: the virtual learner reappeared and said she was fine, then a full debrief** — Slater et al. (2006), same (Procedures) — verified yes, full text yes — "Nothing happened, I'm fine." — For us: a room that hurts a virtual person should show the person unharmed before the text debrief.
- **VR Milgram replication: obedience in VR is higher in absolute terms; 6 of 40 quit; only one prod ("Please continue"), then the experimenter asked if they wanted to quit; psychology students excluded to reduce familiarity** — Gonzalez-Franco et al. (2018), Participant concerns for the Learner in a Virtual Reality replication of the Milgram obedience study, *PLoS ONE* 13(12), e0209704, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0209704 — verified yes, full text yes — "the higher levels of obedience in VR paradigms" — For us: never present VR compliance rates as equal to the original; an agent may prod once, never insist.
- **Violent bystander incident in VR (Cave, 40 male Arsenal fans): in-group victim drew more physical interventions; participants were asked not to talk about the study** — Slater et al. (2013), Bystander responses to a violent incident in an immersive virtual environment, *PLoS ONE* 8(1), e52766, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0052766 — verified yes, full text yes — "asked not to discuss it with others for 3 months" — For us: crosstalk (streamers, friends) is a known threat; ask players not to spoil and ask if they had heard about the room.
- **Bystander VR study warned in advance about aggressive scenes and recruited 18+ only** — Slater et al. (2013), same (Methods) — verified yes, full text yes — "If you are someone who would be put off by witnessing realistic scenes" — For us: distressing rooms need a specific content warning in the consent page of that room.
- **Plausibility failed when agents ignored the participant; lack of interaction was the main complaint** — Slater et al. (2013), same (interviews) — verified yes, full text yes — "the plausibility of the experience would be greatly improved through more interactivity" — For us: scripted agents that never react weaken social effects.
- **Matched real vs VR fire alarm (n = 150, 25 per cell): left the room alone 68% real / 36% VR; passive bystander 12% / 24%; active bystander 92% / 60%** — Kinateder & Warren (2016), Social influence on evacuation behavior in real and virtual environments, *Frontiers in Robotics and AI* 3, 43, doi:10.3389/frobt.2016.00043 — verified yes (Figure 2 counts 17/23/3 and 9/15/6 checked on the PDF page), full text yes — "the negative influence of the passive bystander was weaker in VR" — For us: VR keeps the direction of social influence but shrinks responses; our sources.md numbers are correct.
- **In VR most people assumed the alarm was staged: 68% in VR vs 20% in the real room said it was part of the experiment** — Kinateder & Warren (2016), same (Questionnaire results) — verified yes, full text yes — "only 20% in the real environment but 68% in the virtual environment" — For us: in VR every event is presumed to be the experimenter's; "thought it was part of the test" must be asked and analysed.
- **That study used a cover story and told VR participants the virtual bystander was a recording of the previous participant (deception), with full debrief** — Kinateder & Warren (2016), same (Procedure) — verified yes, full text yes — "told that the virtual human was a recording of the previous participant" — For us: if we ever claim an agent is a real person, that is deception and needs APA 8.07 justification.
- **Asch in immersive VR (Oculus DK2, n = 22): no conformity — 3 wrong answers in 264 trials; only response times changed** — Kyrlitsias & Michael-Grigoriou (2018), Asch conformity experiment using immersive virtual reality, *Computer Animation and Virtual Worlds* e1804, doi:10.1002/cav.1804 (pre-peer-review version read) — verified yes, full text yes (preprint) — "only 3 were given a wrong answer (1.14%)" — For us: an unambiguous Asch task with agents gives almost no conformity.
- **Same paper, Exp. 2 (n = 52): 4 of 52 gave at least one conforming answer; people who knew Asch were excluded** — Kyrlitsias & Michael-Grigoriou (2018), same — verified yes, full text yes (preprint) — "48 of the 52 participants (92.3%) responded correctly to all trials" — For us: "Asch works in VR at ~34%" is not supported by immersive studies.
- **Harder task (5 s exposure) + participants introduced themselves: 63% of 38 conformed at least once; conformity was conscious (matched self-report); gaze of agents did not change it** — Kyrlitsias et al. (2020), Social conformity in immersive virtual environments: The impact of agents' gaze behavior, *Frontiers in Psychology* 11, 2254, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2020.02254 — verified yes, full text yes — "a percentage fairly small, in contrast to 63.16% of this study" — For us: conformity to agents in VR is mostly informational and needs an ambiguous task; this is not Asch's design.
- **The "26–34%" conformity figure comes from a non-peer-reviewed grant report on moral judgements, with a real-human condition included; headset use not stated** — Bocian, Gonidis & Everett (2020), EASP Seedcorn Grant Report, https://easp.eu/news/itm/-1176.html — verified yes (page opened), full text yes (web page) — "The overall conformity ratio for all three conditions was 34.3%" — For us: do not use as a VR norm (see Conflicts).
- **A VR trolley study at home (Quest/Rift, recruited on Reddit/SideQuest): 33 valid participants over the approved period; 3 failed the control scenario; 5 said their logged action was accidental** — Kissel, Rechowicz & Shull (2023), Murder on the VR Express, *Societies* 13, 69, doi:10.3390/soc13030069 — verified yes, full text partly (key passages checked; card written by another session) — "Five participants reported that the action or inaction logged by our experience was accidental." — For us: after a one-shot choice, ask "was that what you meant to do?" before counting it.

## 3. Remote and unsupervised VR studies (home headsets, no experimenter)

- **Out-of-lab VR (cardboard, n = 57) gave the same experimental effects as the lab (HTC Vive, n = 31); absolute performance depended on hardware; body ownership depended more on control** — Mottelson & Hornbæk (2017), Virtual reality studies outside the laboratory, *Proc. VRST '17*, Article 9, doi:10.1145/3139131.3139141 — verified yes, full text yes — "complex VR phenomena depend more critically on the internal control of the study" — For us: compare conditions within our own players; never compare our absolute values with lab values.
- **Exclusion rules used out of lab: zero-variance answers, a control question, completion time > M + 3 SD, too-fast answers; 100 cardboards → 80 installs → 57 completed** — Mottelson & Hornbæk (2017), same — verified yes, full text yes — "Expect roughly half of the participants to complete the study" — For us: plan for large drop-out; preregister exclusion rules of this kind.
- **Consent there was implied by continuing after being told about logging (a weaker standard)** — Mottelson & Hornbæk (2017), same (3.6 Ethical concerns) — verified yes, full text yes — "implicitly get consent from the user by continuing use of the application" — For us: our explicit two-button consent is stronger; keep it.
- **First "in the wild" VR experiment: in-app consent with an 18+ item, a full path for users who refuse data collection, debrief screen showing which condition they were in, data removal via an anonymous device ID** — Steed et al. (2016), An 'in the wild' experiment on presence and embodiment using consumer virtual reality equipment, *IEEE TVCG* 22(4), 1406–1414, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2016.2518135 — verified yes, full text yes — "I confirm that I am over 18 years old and that I consent" — For us: matches our "start without recording" path; add the explicit 18+ confirmation and the "your condition was…" debrief.
- **Unsupervised = no stressful content: the ethics committee ruled out a virtual-pit experience; seated only** — Steed et al. (2016), same (3.5) — verified yes, full text yes — "the content was not shocking" — For us: no fall-risk, startle or high-stress rooms for unsupervised home play.
- **Data yield: ~400 installs → 115 agreed and uploaded → 85 complete → 59 kept (26 rejected: test builds, control question, straight-lining); about 15%** — Steed et al. (2016), same (4.1) — verified yes, full text yes — "This was a rate of return of 15%" — For us: count installs/starts/completions per condition and report them.
- **Home XR studies: researchers' main worries are recruitment, data collection, safety, hardware variability, honesty; XR hardware itself is a data-collection device** — Ratcliffe et al. (2021), Extended Reality (XR) remote research: a survey of drawbacks and opportunities, *CHI '21*, doi:10.1145/3411764.3445170 — verified yes, full text yes — "Debriefing is such a good space to collect unstructured interview data" — For us: build the debrief as a data moment (open question "what did you think it was about?").
- **Home setting cited from Ma et al. 2018 (not read): 98% took part at home, 84% alone** — Ratcliffe et al. (2021), same (2.2) — verified yes (as reported), full text yes — "98% of participants took part at home" — For us: assume a home room, possibly others nearby; design for seated/standing.
- **Unsupervised Quest studies online: in-VR questionnaires reliable (Cronbach α ≈ .80–.87, comparable to lab), but ~13% inattentive online vs 3% in the lab** — Mottelson et al. (2021), Conducting unsupervised virtual reality user studies online, *Frontiers in Virtual Reality* 2, 681482, doi:10.3389/frvir.2021.681482 — verified yes, full text yes — "about 13% of the cohort did not pay an ideal amount of attention" — For us: preregister an objective attention/validity criterion; expect ~10–13% loss.
- **Hand tracking at home needs a screening phase: bad tracking fell from 27% to 8% after adding one** — Mottelson et al. (2021), same (Study II) — verified yes, full text yes — "The screening test notably lowered the data rejection rate from 27 to 8%." — For us: any hand-tracking room starts with a tracking-quality check (light, hand in view).
- **Checklist for unsupervised VR: log HMD removal, measure duration and flag outliers, report drop-outs per condition, check answer consistency, define exclusions before data, ask about distractions** — Mottelson et al. (2021), same (Data Quality) — verified yes, full text yes — "Estimate about 10% aberrant responses" — For us: our planned pause log matches; add "were you interrupted?" and drop-out per condition.
- **Home Quest owners are young, male, VR-expert: 86% male, median age 26** — Mottelson et al. (2021), same (Demographics) — verified yes, full text yes — "claims about generalizability should be avoided" — For us: "you vs other players", never "you vs people"; report demographics with every result.
- **Home VR vs lab vs reality (pedestrian gap acceptance): results similar, but far more data cleaning at home (19 excluded crossing times vs 2 in lab)** — Maruhn (2021), VR pedestrian simulator studies at home, *Frontiers in Virtual Reality* 2, 746971, doi:10.3389/frvir.2021.746971 — verified yes, full text partly (key passages checked; card by another session) — "more data points had to be cleaned under the remote condition" — For us: expect noisier home data; cleaning rules must be fixed in advance.

## 4. Ethics: codes and VR-specific rules

- **APA 8.02: consent tells purpose, duration, procedures; right to decline and withdraw; consequences; foreseeable risks/discomfort; benefits; limits of confidentiality; incentives; whom to contact** — American Psychological Association (2017), Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct (2002, amended 2010, 2016), https://www.apa.org/ethics/code/ethics-code-2017.pdf — verified yes, full text yes (standards 8.01–8.10 read) — "their right to decline to participate and to withdraw" — For us: our consent lacks duration, risks (sickness) and contact on the sheet (contact only on the privacy page).
- **APA 8.07: deception only if justified by significant value and no non-deceptive alternative; never about pain or severe distress; explained no later than end of data collection, and participants may withdraw their data** — APA (2017), same — verified yes, full text yes — "permit participants to withdraw their data" — For us: after the reveal the player must be able to discard the run; today the run is sent before the reveal (see Conflicts).
- **APA 8.08: prompt debriefing; correct misconceptions; if procedures harmed someone, minimise the harm** — APA (2017), same — verified yes, full text yes — "take reasonable steps to correct any misconceptions" — For us: the reveal is our 8.08 debrief; it must be reachable even after early exit (we have left-early.js).
- **APA 8.05: consent may be dispensed with only for anonymous, harmless questionnaires and similar** — APA (2017), same — verified yes, full text yes — "only anonymous questionnaires, naturalistic observations, or archival research" — For us: a behavioural room with withheld details always needs consent.
- **BPS: withholding details ≠ lying about purpose; deception is inappropriate if the reveal is likely to anger participants** — British Psychological Society (2021), BPS Code of Human Research Ethics, ISBN 978-1-85433-792-4 (section 7) — verified yes, full text partly (sections 1–7, 10 read; review-procedure chapters skipped) — "likely to lead to discomfort, anger or objections from the participants" — For us: pilot every reveal for anger; never give a false, more benign purpose.
- **BPS: if information is withheld, the information sheet must say so and how it will be given at the end** — BPS (2021), same (4.1) — verified yes, full text partly — "the means by which the withheld information will be given" — For us: our line "Часть деталей мы раскроем только в конце" meets this.
- **BPS: time limits on data withdrawal must be stated in the consent process** — BPS (2021), same (2.1) — verified yes, full text partly — "these limits should always be made clear to participants" — For us: the consent sheet itself (not only privacy.html) must say an anonymous run cannot be removed after sending.
- **BPS: research that may lead participants to label themselves ("I am stupid") or that induces stress is more than minimal risk** — BPS (2021), same (3. Risk) — verified yes, full text partly — "Research that may lead to `labelling'" — For us: keeps our rule "you did X, never a diagnosis".
- **BPS: after a negative-mood manipulation, restore mood before the participant leaves** — BPS (2021), same (10. Debriefing) — verified yes, full text partly — "it would be ethical to induce a happy mood state" — For us: rooms that embarrass or stress the player end on a positive, normalising note.
- **BPS: pilot the information and the debrief with a naive person of lower literacy** — BPS (2021), same (4.1) — verified yes, full text partly — "at least one pilot test of the processes for informing and debriefing" — For us: playtest the consent and the reveal texts, not only the room.
- **BPS internet-mediated research: a "Quit study" button that leads to a debrief; ask whether partial data may be used; a final button confirming consent to use the data** — BPS (2021), Ethics guidelines for internet-mediated research, ISBN 978-1-85433-796-2 — verified yes, full text yes — "A button at the very end of a study confirming consent" — For us: add "keep / discard my result" after the reveal; our exit sign + left-early flow already gives quit → debrief.
- **BPS IMR: IP addresses collected automatically should be used only to check multiple completions and then deleted; for retrospective withdrawal ask for a memorable code** — BPS (2021) IMR, same (Anonymity) — verified yes, full text yes — "ask participants to provide a memorable code or ID number" — For us: if we ever allow later withdrawal, a player-chosen code is the sanctioned way.
- **BPS IMR: age cannot be verified online; for adult themes consider offline; in low risk send under-age entries to an exit page with no re-entry** — BPS (2021) IMR, same (Principle 4) — verified yes, full text yes — "an exit page from which they are unable to re-enter" — For us: turn "Участвовать можно с 18 лет" into an age question with an exit for recording.
- **VR code of conduct (Madary & Metzinger): no experiment with foreseeable serious or lasting harm; consent should state that immersive VR can have lasting behavioural influences; no VR research on people unable to consent** — Madary & Metzinger (2016), Real virtuality: A code of ethical conduct, *Frontiers in Robotics and AI* 3, 3, doi:10.3389/frobt.2016.00003 — verified yes, full text yes — "immersive VR can have lasting behavioral influences on subjects" — For us: one consent line about possible after-effects is the VR-specific addition we lack.
- **Madary & Metzinger: users must be reminded inside VR that recording is on (e.g., a small symbol while data are collected)** — Madary & Metzinger (2016), same (VR research and the internet; Table 1, 5b) — verified yes, full text yes — "Users should be repeatedly reminded within VR that they have given informed consent." — For us: show a small recording mark in the scene while a recorded run is in progress.
- **Madary & Metzinger: VR suffering is real suffering; screen out people vulnerable to dissociation for intense embodiment work** — Madary & Metzinger (2016), same — verified yes, full text yes — "Torture in a virtual environment is still torture." — For us: no room may rely on real distress; body-swap rooms need an exclusion note.
- **Madary & Metzinger: movement data are a "kinematic fingerprint" and threaten privacy** — Madary & Metzinger (2016), same — verified yes, full text yes — "there will a highly individual "kinematic fingerprint."" — For us: raw head/hand trajectories are never "anonymous"; send derived numbers only.

## 5. Debriefing: does it work, how to do it well

- **A standard debriefing does not undo a manipulation: after a typical debrief 75% and 63% of those misled still endorsed the false details 5 days later** — Greenspan & Loftus (2022), What happens after debriefing? The effectiveness and benefits of postexperimental debriefing, *Memory & Cognition* 50, 696–709, doi:10.3758/s13421-021-01223-9 — verified yes, full text yes — "after debriefing, some participants continued to endorse misinformation" — For us: a one-line "it was a trick" is not enough.
- **An enhanced debrief that names the specific manipulation, explains how the error happens and says such errors are normal removed the effect for the named item and improved the experience; it worked online too** — Greenspan & Loftus (2022), same (Study 2, N = 439) — verified yes, full text yes — "the misinformation effect postdebriefing was eliminated" — For us: each reveal names exactly what was done to the player, why it works, and that it is normal.
- **Online debriefs were split into small pages with a minimum time per page; longer debriefs did not reduce attention** — Greenspan & Loftus (2022), same — verified yes, full text yes — "participants were required to stay on each page for several seconds" — For us: matches our one-thought-per-sheet clipboard; add a short minimum dwell on key reveal pages.
- **Debriefed participants report learning more; some withdraw consent after the debrief (9 of ~400 and 5 of ~440)** — Greenspan & Loftus (2022), same (Participants) — verified yes, full text yes — "nine participants for withdrawing their consent after debriefing" — For us: a real share of people want out after the reveal; we must offer it before sending.
- **Ego-threat debriefings only partly work; effects lasted 2+ weeks (Miketta & Friese 2019, JPSP 117, 282–309, doi:10.1037/pspa0000155)** — summary seen only via search results and as cited by Greenspan & Loftus — verified no (full text not obtained), full text no — no quote — For us: avoid ego-threatening feedback (fake test scores) in rooms.

## 6. Consent in VR, minimum age, anonymity and GDPR

- **Consent inside VR is feasible: Steed put posters, a voiceover and an 18+ consent item in the app; Mottelson 2021 took consent in the browser before install and saw no technical obstacle to doing it in VR** — Steed et al. (2016); Mottelson et al. (2021) — verified yes, full text yes — "no technical reason why informed consent could not be prompted upon opening the application" — For us: our clipboard consent in the corridor is in line with practice; no study found tests comprehension of consent read inside an HMD (see Gaps).
- **Online consent: a designated consent page with explicit statements, consent at start and at end, not overlong** — BPS (2021) IMR (Valid consent) — verified yes, full text yes — "Overly lengthy consent information pages are actually likely to be counterproductive" — For us: keep the two short pages; add an end-of-room confirmation.
- **Meta accounts: 10–12 only with a parent-managed account (Quest 2 and 3), 13+ own account (ages vary by country), under 10 not allowed** — Meta Help Center, Why you need to have the right Meta account for your age, https://www.meta.com/en-gb/help/quest/734738691458324/ and Meta Quest parent info, https://www.meta.com/quest/parent-info/ — verified yes (pages opened), full text yes — "Children must be at least 10 years old to create a Meta Account" — For us: children aged 10–17 can legitimately open our site in a headset; content must be safe for them even if they ignore "18+".
- **BPS: 16 is acceptable for sole consent in low-risk research; safeguarding duties remain until 18** — BPS (2021) Code (4.5) — verified yes, full text partly — "the age of 16 should be acceptable for sole consent" — For us: 18+ for recording is stricter than BPS minimum and matches Steed; keep it.
- **GDPR Art. 8: consent of a child to online services lawful from 16; states may lower it, not below 13** — Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Art. 8(1), https://gdpr-info.eu/art-8-gdpr/ — verified yes (text opened), full text yes (article) — "provided that such lower age is not below 13 years" — For us: one more reason to keep recording 18+ (or at least 16+).
- **GDPR Recital 26: truly anonymous information is outside GDPR; identifiability judged by means reasonably likely to be used** — Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Recital 26, https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-26/ — verified yes, full text yes (recital) — "The principles of data protection should therefore not apply to anonymous information" — For us: our anonymity claim must survive "means reasonably likely" (cost, time, technology).
- **GDPR Art. 89: research processing needs appropriate safeguards** — Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Art. 89(1), https://gdpr-info.eu/art-89-gdpr/ — verified yes, full text yes (article) — "shall be subject to appropriate safeguards" — For us: the future science use (after ethics approval) needs a written data-safeguard plan.
- **Anonymisation must block singling out, linkability and inference; pseudonymised data (e.g., hashed IDs) are not anonymous** — Article 29 Working Party (2014), Opinion 05/2014 on Anonymisation Techniques (WP216), https://ec.europa.eu/justice/article-29/documentation/opinion-recommendation/files/2014/wp216_en.pdf — verified yes, full text partly (executive summary and criteria sections) — "pseudonymisation is not a method of anonymisation" — For us: no device ID or hash in results; publish only aggregates with enough runs per cell (age group × sex × room × version can single people out).
- **Methods advice to store a hashed device ID to stop repeat participation conflicts with anonymity** — Mottelson et al. (2021) (Data Quality) vs WP29 (2014) — verified yes, full text yes / partly — "a hash of the identifier could be used instead" — For us: keep our local first/repeat flag (nothing sent) and accept imperfect repeat detection.

## 7. Demand characteristics and suspicion probes

- **Every experiment has demand characteristics; participants try to be "good subjects" and to guess the hypothesis** — Orne (1962), On the social psychology of the psychological experiment: With particular reference to demand characteristics and their implications, *American Psychologist* 17, 776–783, doi:10.1037/h0043424 — verified yes, full text yes — "it is futile to imagine an experiment that could be created without demand characteristics" — For us: measure what each player thought the room was about.
- **Inquiry must start open ("What do you think the experiment is about?"), encourage guessing, and beware the "pact of ignorance" between experimenter and participant** — Orne (1962), same — verified yes, full text yes — "it is commonplace to find a pact of ignorance" — For us: the probe asks open before specific, and accepts no "I don't know" without a guess.
- **Release forms and a "panic button" alone produced sensory-deprivation-like effects (Orne & Scheibe, described by Orne)** — Orne (1962), same — verified yes (as described there), full text yes — "the presence of a "panic (release) button"" — For us: warnings, exit sign and consent wording are demand cues; they must be identical across conditions of a room.
- **Pre-experimental inquiry and simulating controls estimate how much of an effect demand alone can produce** — Orne (1962), same — verified yes, full text yes — "This technique, which we have termed the preexperimental inquiry" — For us: before publishing a room's effect, run a "describe what would happen" control with naive players.
- **Post-experimental inquiries miss much suspicion: even with improvements, only 36 of 69 participants told the trick beforehand scored 4–5 on awareness; prior information was rarely admitted** — Blackhart et al. (2012), Assessing the adequacy of postexperimental inquiries in deception research and the factors that promote participant honesty, *Behavior Research Methods* 44, 24–40, doi:10.3758/s13428-011-0132-6 — verified yes, full text yes — "Nearly half of the participants with compromising information about the study did not reveal" — For us: our probe will under-count suspicion; treat it as a lower bound.
- **Practice among 77 deception researchers: 84% ask before the debrief; 55% use a funnel; 58% drop fully aware participants; a computer survey with a scientific-integrity statement and a minimum time modestly helped** — Blackhart et al. (2012), same (Studies 1–3) — verified yes, full text yes — "most researchers ask participants what they thought the study was about" — For us: self-administered in-VR probe before the reveal, with an integrity statement ("your answer never changes your result").
- **Why people hide suspicion: fear of ruining the study (44%), losing credit (39%), getting others in trouble (35%); a reward for guessing raised admitted awareness but lowered admission of prior information** — Blackhart et al. (2012), same (Studies 3–4) — verified yes, full text yes — "participants are most concerned with ruining the study" — For us: word the "did you know it?" question so that knowing is fine and nobody is blamed; no prize for guessing.
- **In VR people assume events are staged (68% vs 20%), and VR Milgram/Asch studies excluded or measured those who knew the classic study** — Kinateder & Warren (2016); Slater et al. (2006); Kyrlitsias et al. (2018, 2020); Gonzalez-Franco et al. (2018) — verified yes, full text yes — "two participants were excluded as they were aware of the original Asch experiment" — For us: probe items: "Did you think any of this was staged?" and "Did you know this experiment or see someone play it?".

## 8. Measurement on consumer headsets

- **Quest 2 hand tracking: mean fingertip error 1.1 cm (0.79 cm central vs 1.3 cm peripheral), joint-angle error 9.6°, delay 45 ms; tracking unpredictable above ~3.2 m/s; only light skin tones tested; run over Link on a PC** — Abdlkarim et al. (2024), A methodological framework to assess the accuracy of virtual reality hand-tracking systems: A case study with the Meta Quest 2, *Behavior Research Methods* 56, 1052–1063, doi:10.3758/s13428-022-02051-8 — verified yes, full text yes — "an average fingertip positional error of 1.1cm" — For us: hand-tracking rooms can measure where a hand is to ~1 cm with ~45 ms lag; not fit for millisecond motor timing; keep hands central.
- **Quest 3 head tracking: mean relative pose error 0.79 cm and absolute pose error 7.99 cm; absolute error over 10 cm in dim, featureless rooms; slow head rotation best** — Hu et al. (2024), Apple vs. Meta: A comparative study on spatial tracking in SOTA XR headsets, *ACM MobiCom '24*, doi:10.1145/3636534.3696215 — verified yes, full text yes — "they exhibited APE exceeding 10 cm in challenging scenarios" — For us: head position is fine for "where did you look/step" but drifts by several cm; measure distances relative, not absolute; dim home rooms degrade it.
- **Native VR engine timing: built-in reaction-time capture was off by a mean +55 ms with a 49–61 ms range (once-per-frame input, display delay); a predicted-display-time method fixed it to ~1.4 ms** — Wiesing, Fink & Weidner (2020), Accuracy and precision of stimulus timing and reaction times with Unreal Engine and SteamVR, *PLoS ONE* 15(4), e0231152, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0231152 — verified yes, full text yes — "a mean measurement error of +55 ms" — For us: WebXR timestamps are also frame-bound; RT differences below a frame or two are not measurable without validation; drop trials with dropped frames.
- **Browser experiments: response-time records carry ~70–120 ms delays depending on browser/device; use within-participant comparisons** — Anwyl-Irvine et al. (2021), Realistic precision and accuracy of online experiment platforms, web browsers, and devices, *Behavior Research Methods* 53, 1407–1425, doi:10.3758/s13428-020-01501-5 — verified yes, full text partly (abstract, results summaries, discussion; not Quest Browser) — "we recommend employing within-participant designs where possible" — For us: RT rooms compare conditions within the same player and headset.
- **Eye tracking on Meta headsets is supported on Quest Pro (and Meta VR Glasses), not Quest 3** — Meta Horizon developer docs, Eye tracking, https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/unity/move-eye-tracking/ — verified yes (page opened), full text partly (first 100k characters) — no quote (page summarised by the fetch tool) — For us: confirms our headset note; gaze = head direction only.
- **Quest Pro eye tracking (n = 78): median spatial accuracy about 1.08° on a bright background; worse for a share of users and after slippage** — Aziz et al. (2024), Evaluation of eye tracking signal quality for virtual reality applications: A case study in the Meta Quest Pro, arXiv:2403.07210 — verified yes, full text partly (abstract, method, results numbers) — "U50|E50 = 1.08 for RAN127" — For us: even where eye tracking exists, design for the worse 25% of users; not relevant on Quest 3.
- **SSQ: 16 symptoms in three subscales (nausea, oculomotor, disorientation), derived from >1,100 questionnaires on 10 Navy flight simulators; scoring intended for post-exposure symptoms, with unwell people screened out** — Kennedy, Lane, Berbaum & Lilienthal (1993), Simulator Sickness Questionnaire: An enhanced method for quantifying simulator sickness, *International Journal of Aviation Psychology* 3(3), 203–220, doi:10.1207/s15327108ijap0303_3 — verified yes, full text partly (abstract, method, factor results; scan with OCR) — "intended only for application to postexposure symptoms" — For us: a "short sickness check" is not the SSQ; if used, take the full 16 items after the room plus a "were you unwell today?" item.
- **VRSQ: SSQ revised for VR from 24 users doing target selection** — Kim, Park, Choi & Choe (2018), Virtual reality sickness questionnaire (VRSQ), *Applied Ergonomics* 69, 66–73, doi:10.1016/j.apergo.2017.12.016 — verified yes (PubMed record), full text no (abstract only) — "24 users performed target selection tasks" — For us: small development sample; not adopted until read in full.
- **IPQ presence: three subscales (spatial presence, involvement, experienced realism) plus one general item; free; German/English/Dutch; authors now advise the full item list, a 7-point −3..+3 scale, and cross-validation of each translation** — igroup.org, igroup presence questionnaire (IPQ) overview (Schubert, Friedmann & Regenbrecht 2001, *Presence* 10(3), 266–281, not read), http://www.igroup.org/pq/ipq/index.php — verified yes (page opened), full text yes (page) — "always use the full item list of the IPQ" — For us: no validated Russian IPQ is listed; a Russian version would need validation before science use.

## 9. For us: what home results need to become science; consent/debrief/anonymity standards; what we must never do

- **Usable later as science** — sources: Mottelson et al. 2021; Steed et al. 2016; Mottelson & Hornbæk 2017; Blackhart et al. 2012; Orne 1962; APA 8.07; BPS IMR — verified yes, full text yes — "Define objective exclusion criteria before collecting data" — For us: (1) preregister outcome, exclusions (duration ±3 SD, control item, attention, interruptions, "accidental" choice), analysis; (2) log room version, condition, first/repeat, prior knowledge, suspicion, seated/standing, headset, browser, frame drops, HMD-off time; (3) report installs/starts/completions per condition; (4) analyse suspicion and prior knowledge as moderators; (5) report sample demographics and never generalise beyond players.
- **Consent standard for our flow** — sources: APA 8.02; BPS 4.1, 2.1; BPS IMR; Madary & Metzinger; Steed et al. — verified yes, full text yes/partly — "Any debriefing that is planned" (BPS 4.1 list) — For us: consent must name: it's a psychology experiment; some details withheld until the end; how long it takes; possible discomfort (sickness) and possible after-effects; quit any time and how; what is recorded and that an anonymous run cannot be deleted after it is sent; 18+ to record (asked, not just stated); contact. Then a recording mark during play, and a final keep/discard choice after the reveal.
- **Debrief standard** — sources: APA 8.08; Greenspan & Loftus 2022; BPS 10; Slater et al. 2006; Pan & Hamilton 2018 — verified yes, full text yes — "Psychologists provide a prompt opportunity for participants to obtain appropriate information" — For us: probe first (open → specific), then name exactly what was done and why, why it works, that it is normal, what the original found, "your condition was…", mood repair, keep/discard, "please don't spoil it for others".
- **Never** — sources: APA 8.07(b); BPS 7; Madary & Metzinger; Steed et al.; BPS IMR; WP29 — verified yes, full text yes/partly — "reasonably expected to cause physical pain or severe emotional distress" — For us: never deceive about pain/severe distress; never state a false benign purpose; never pressure a player who wants to stop (one neutral prompt at most); never run fall-risk, startle or high-stress content unsupervised at home; never send data before the player could withdraw after the reveal; never call pseudonymous or motion data "anonymous"; never reward guessing the hypothesis; never use partial data from a player who quit without asking.

## Conflicts with our notes

1. **`docs/roadmap.md` (Principles, "VR is valid for this"): "Asch in VR (conformity about 34%)"** and **`objekt-files/notes/archive/docs/experiments.md`: "Weaker with virtual agents: about 26–34%"** — not supported. The 26–34% comes from a non-peer-reviewed EASP grant report (moral judgements; 34.3% is the average over three conditions including real humans; headset use not stated). Immersive Asch studies found 1.14% wrong trials and no conformity (Kyrlitsias & Michael-Grigoriou 2018, Exp. 1), 4 of 52 people conforming at least once (Exp. 2), and 63% of 38 conforming at least once only when the task was made hard (5 s exposure) (Kyrlitsias et al. 2020). The roadmap row also cites Slater 2006 and Kinateder 2016 as sources for the Asch number; neither is about Asch.
2. **`docs/roadmap.md`: "People react to virtual social situations as if real"** — overstated. Slater et al. 2006: similar pattern "at lesser intensity" (and a Cave, not an HMD); Kinateder & Warren 2016: 36% vs 68% left alone, passive-bystander effect weaker in VR, and 68% of VR participants thought the alarm was part of the experiment; Gonzalez-Franco et al. 2018: obedience higher in VR. Better wording: "VR keeps the direction of many social effects, with different (often smaller) size; check per room."
3. **Results are sent before the debrief.** `src/rooms/01-control/room.js` `reveal()` calls `session.finish(...)` before the reveal pages; `privacy.html` says sent runs cannot be deleted. APA 8.07(c) requires letting participants withdraw their data once the withheld part is explained; BPS IMR recommends a final consent-to-use button; Greenspan & Loftus saw people withdraw after debrief. Fix (for the owner to decide): send only after a "keep / discard my result" choice at the end of the reveal; state on the consent sheet that after sending it cannot be removed (BPS 2.1).
4. **`docs/methodology.md`: "Funnel debriefing / suspicion probe (Blackhart et al. 2012)"** — the citation is right (doi checked) but the paper shows that such probes miss about half of the suspicion/prior knowledge and that rewards backfire for prior information. The note should say the probe is a lower bound, self-administered, with an integrity statement and blame-free wording, no reward; and add Orne 1962 for open-first, encourage-guessing questioning.
5. **Consent text (`src/app/texts.ru.js`): "Участвовать можно с 18 лет."** is a statement, not a check. Steed used an explicit "I confirm I am over 18" item; BPS IMR recommends an age question with an exit page. Meta allows 10–12 (parent-managed) and 13+ accounts on Quest 2/3, so minors can be in Quest Browser.
6. **Consent content gaps vs APA 8.02 / BPS 4.1 / Madary**: no duration, no mention of discomfort (sickness), no VR after-effects line, contact only on the privacy page, the "cannot be deleted" limit only on the privacy page; no in-VR recording reminder during play (Madary & Metzinger).
7. **`docs/methodology.md`: "VR reporting (Kennedy et al. 1993 SSQ) … optional short sickness check"** — SSQ was built on Navy flight-simulator data and is scored on post-exposure symptoms with unwell people screened out; a shortened check is not the SSQ. VRSQ exists (24-user development; abstract only).
8. **`docs/roadmap.md` Data row cites presencis.com for GDPR Recital 26** — a third-party site; cite the regulation text and WP29 Opinion 05/2014. "Truly anonymous" must also hold against singling out by rare combinations (age group + sex + room + version + condition + first/repeat) and against linkage with provider IP logs (`privacy.html` says Supabase/Cloudflare may log IPs).
9. **`docs/roadmap.md` Deception row: "Allowed only with a full debriefing right after; the player can quit at any time"** — incomplete. APA 8.07 adds: justified by significant value, no non-deceptive alternative, never about pain/severe distress, data withdrawal after the explanation; BPS 7 adds: inappropriate if the reveal is likely to cause anger, and never a false benign purpose.
10. **`objekt-files/notes/archive/docs/experiments.md` Obedience row "VR version Slater 2006 … Proven in VR"** — Slater 2006 used a Cave, studied presence (not obedience), had no deception, and 17 of 23 (74%) went to the end, above Milgram's 65%; Gonzalez-Franco 2018 confirms higher absolute obedience in VR. "Proven" should read "reproduces the pattern with different levels".
11. **`docs/headset-capabilities.md` "Eye tracking: not on Quest 3" had no source** — now sourced: Meta's eye-tracking doc lists Quest Pro (and Meta VR Glasses), not Quest 3. Not a conflict; add the link.

## Top 15 for our game (ranked)

1. **Keep/discard after the reveal, then send** — APA 8.07(c); BPS IMR; Greenspan & Loftus 2022. Today the run is sent before the reveal.
2. **Suspicion and prior-knowledge probe before every reveal** — open question first, encourage a guess, then "anything odd / staged?", "did you know this experiment or watch someone play it?"; self-administered, blame-free, no reward; analyse as moderator — Orne 1962; Blackhart et al. 2012; Kinateder & Warren 2016.
3. **Enhanced reveal** — name exactly what was done, why it works, that it is normal, what the original found, your condition; mood repair; short pages with a minimum dwell — Greenspan & Loftus 2022; BPS 10; Steed et al. 2016.
4. **Withhold, never lie** — consent says details come at the end (done); no false benign purpose; no deception about pain or severe distress; pilot reveals for anger — BPS 7, 4.1; APA 8.07(a,b).
5. **Preregistered exclusions + home-data logging** — duration outliers, control/attention item, HMD-off/interruptions, "was that intentional?", per-condition drop-out; expect ~10–15% loss — Mottelson et al. 2021; Steed et al. 2016; Kissel et al. 2023.
6. **VR numbers are VR numbers** — present "in VR, players…", compare conditions within our players, never equate with the original's percentages — Kinateder & Warren 2016; Slater et al. 2006; Gonzalez-Franco et al. 2018; Kyrlitsias et al. 2018/2020.
7. **No stressful, startle or fall-risk content unsupervised** — seated/standing, content warnings per room — Steed et al. 2016; Ratcliffe et al. 2021; BPS IMR; Slater et al. 2013.
8. **Age question with exit for recording (18+), content safe for 10–17-year-olds** — Meta age rules; BPS IMR; BPS 4.5; GDPR Art. 8.
9. **Anonymity done properly** — no IDs or hashes, no raw motion/voice, aggregate-only publication with minimum cell sizes, honest wording about provider IP logs — WP29 2014; Madary & Metzinger 2016; BPS IMR.
10. **Complete the consent** — duration, discomfort, possible after-effects, quit how, what is recorded, deletion limit, contact; plus an in-VR recording mark — APA 8.02; BPS 4.1/2.1; Madary & Metzinger.
11. **Demand cues identical across conditions** — consent, warnings, exit sign, experimenter voice the same in every condition; run a "predict what happens" control before claiming an effect — Orne 1962.
12. **First vs repeat and crosstalk** — keep the local first/repeat flag; ask players not to spoil; ask "did you see someone play it?" — Slater et al. 2013; Blackhart et al. 2012; Kyrlitsias et al. 2018.
13. **Report the sample** — home Quest owners are young, male, VR-experienced; say "other players", report demographics — Mottelson et al. 2021.
14. **Measurement limits by design** — head direction only (no eye tracking on Quest 3); hand position ~1 cm/45 ms; head absolute position drifts several cm; RT only within-player and only after validating Quest Browser timing — Meta docs; Abdlkarim et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2024; Wiesing et al. 2020; Anwyl-Irvine et al. 2021.
15. **Questionnaires used as published** — SSQ full 16 items post-exposure with an "unwell today?" screen; IPQ full list; no unvalidated Russian versions counted as science — Kennedy et al. 1993; igroup.org.
