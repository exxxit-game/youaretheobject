# 10. The floor directory by the stairwell door (US, 1975-1980)

Question: what a visitor saw next to the stairs of a 10+ storey US university building around
1975-1980: the sign listing the floors and what is on each. Time-boxed research (about 10
minutes, 10.10.2026). Marks as in `07-corridor-1979.md`: **opened**, **search**, **unverified**.

## 1. Form

- **Changeable letter directory board.** Tablet & Ticket Co. (Chicago) registered a trademark
  in 1948 for "building directory boards and changeable letter signs" [F1, search]. So the
  changeable-letter building directory existed well before 1979.
- Modern catalogs still sell the same build: felt grooved background (also cork or magnetic),
  extruded aluminum frame 1-3/8 in face, 2 in deep, surface or recessed mount (Nelson-Harkins
  240 series) [F2, opened]; felt grooved on 1/4 in centres for single-tab white injection-moulded
  letters; enclosed cabinets with locking door and glass or acrylic window (Aarco) [F3, search].
  These are modern pages: that the 1979 board looked the same is **unverified** until a period
  catalog or photo is found.

- **A period photo, 1970 (found 10.10, opened)**: George Skip Gandy IV, "Display Board with Company Names",
  1 Oct 1970, Tampa (USF Digital Commons, Gandy 29_0750, CC BY-NC 3.0),
  https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/gandy_commercial/10844 : a weathered wooden board on a white wall, the names
  on dark embossed label-tape strips (Dymo-style) with numbers. A commercial building, not a university one.

## 2. Sizes, colours, mounting

- Frame and grooves: see 1 (1-3/8 in aluminum face, 2 in deep; grooves on 1/4 in centres;
  finishes clear satin, black, dark bronze) [F2, F3; modern]. One modern enclosed felt board
  is 24 x 18 in, 2 in deep [F8, search; modern]. Board sizes of 1979: **unverified**.
- Letters: modern single-tab plastic sets come 1/2, 3/4, 1, 2 and 3 in high [F9, search;
  modern]. A seller's box of 1970s Demco display letters (not directory letters) holds 3/4,
  1-1/8 and 2 in [F9, search]. Letter heights on a 1979 directory: **unverified**.
- Colour: black or dark felt with white letters is what modern sellers offer as standard; for
  1979 **unverified**. ANSI A117.1-1980 / UFAS 4.30.3 only asks for light-on-dark or the reverse.
- Mounting height and place relative to the stair door: **unverified** (no period source). The
  nearest period rule is NYC's: the floor number on the stair side of the door, the stair letter
  on the corridor side (27-392, 27-393), and at the elevator a floor diagram sign at least 10 x
  12 in, top no higher than 6 ft (27-391; size and height from the search summary, **search**).

## 3. Stairwell floor identification (standards)

- Later NFPA 101 (2009, 7.2.2.5.4.1): a sign at each floor landing inside enclosed stairs
  serving five or more storeys (new: three or more), giving floor level, top and bottom terminus,
  stair identification, and the level and direction of exit discharge; readable with the door
  open or closed [F4, search]. IBC-based local codes add 18 x 12 in minimum, floor number at
  least 5 in high [F5, search]. Whether NFPA 101-1976 had this rule: **unverified** (no text of
  the 1973 or 1976 edition found online).
- **New York City, 1973-74 (a period text, opened).** Department of Buildings Directive #10 of
  1974, dated May 23, 1974, "Stair and elevator identification signs, Local Law #5/1973": the
  signs of Building Code Sec. C26-608.4 "shall apply on every floor including the street
  (entrance) floor", each sign giving "the alphabetic letter of the elevator bank or stair" [F6,
  opened]. The same rules as now codified (Admin Code 27-391 to 27-396, the 1968 code family):
  inside each stair enclosure on every floor, "on the stair side of the door", the floor number
  "at least three inches high" in bold type, contrasting colours (27-392); the stair letter on
  the occupancy side of the stair door, at least 3 in high (27-393); at elevator landings a
  sign with a floor diagram showing the stairs and their letters (27-391); buildings existing
  before 18 January 1973 got relief, all had to comply by 1 October 1985 (27-396) [F7, opened].
  This is NYC law only; whether a 1979 Illinois building had such signs: **unverified**.
- **Accessibility (ANSI A117.1-1980, as carried into UFAS, 7 Aug 1984).** No clause names
  directories or stair signs. 4.1.2(15): "If signs are provided, they shall comply with 4.30.1,
  4.30.2 and 4.30.3" (so a directory needs only proportion and contrast, not raised letters);
  4.30.3: light characters on a dark background or the reverse; 4.30.2: width-to-height 3:5 to
  1:1, stroke 1:5 to 1:10; raised characters (5/8 to 2 in, raised 1/32 in, sans serif, 4.30.4)
  and latch-side mounting at 54-66 in (4.30.6) only for signs naming rooms and spaces; elevator
  hoistway jambs carry raised floor numbers centred at 60 in (4.10.5) [C3 in 07, re-opened].

## What it settles for us

- Two real forms existed: the changeable-letter directory board (made in the US since at least
  1948, F1) and the stair floor/identification sign (NYC law from 1973, F6). A directory listing
  every floor by the stair door is not required by any period text found; its look (sizes,
  colours, layout) rests on modern catalogs only, so before building it the owner should get
  options, or a period photo should be found first.
- A 1979 Evanston building was not bound by NYC law or by UFAS (1984, federal buildings); its
  stair signs in 1979: **unverified**.
