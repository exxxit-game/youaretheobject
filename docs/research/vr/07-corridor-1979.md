# 07. The corridor as a 1979 US university corridor

Question: what each part of the lobby corridor looked like, and where it was placed, in a
typical US university building of the late 1970s (room 01 recreates Alloy & Abramson 1979,
Northwestern University, Evanston, Illinois). Time-boxed research (about 15 minutes of work).
Starting point: `docs/building-standards.md`.

## 1. Interior office and lab doors

**Hardware: round knob, not lever.**
- Schlage's D-Series cylindrical lock (the common US commercial lock) was made from 1940; lever
  versions came only in 1989: "Schlage introduced the lever handle D-Series locks" [C1, opened].
- Schlage's first key-in-lever lock, the Olympiad, came out in 1983 and was dropped in 1990
  [C2, search]. Schlage later made a retrofit lever for existing D knobs (Orbit, Plymouth,
  Tulip) "when ADA requirements became required" [C2, search].
- Why levers came: the accessibility standards. The first written hardware rule we found is in
  UFAS 1984, which follows ANSI A117.1-1980 in format: hardware must not need tight grasping,
  pinching or twisting of the wrist; "Lever-operated mechanisms, push-type mechanisms, and
  U-shaped handles are acceptable designs"; no hardware for passage higher than 48 in [C3,
  opened]. The ADA rules of 1991 kept the same shape rule [C4, search]. So levers became the
  norm in the 1980s–1990s; in 1979 a university office or lab door had a round knob.
- Knob design of 1979 (Orbit vs Plymouth vs Tulip, finish): **unverified**, no dated catalog
  found.

**Closer.** LCN's 4010/4110 series (regular arm and parallel arm) dates from 1958 [C5, search],
so a surface closer of that family is right for 1979. Which arm was usual on office doors:
**unverified**.

**Frame, hinges, kick plate, vision window.** No period source found. Hollow metal frames and
butt hinges are what we built from today's Steelcraft data; whether 1979 office doors had kick
plates or a narrow vision light: **unverified**.

## 2. Room number and name signs

- **Placement rule history.** The rule "on the wall beside the latch side" first appears in the
  sources we opened in UFAS (Federal Register, 7 August 1984), which follows ANSI A117.1-1980:
  "Interior signage shall be located alongside the door on the latch side", mounted "between
  54 in and 66 in" above the floor; raised sans-serif characters 5/8 to 2 in high [C3, opened].
  The 1991 ADA Standards changed it to the wall at the latch side, 60 in to the sign's
  centreline [C6, search]. The 48–60 in baseline band we use comes from the 2010 ADA Standards
  (703.4.1) [C6, search].
- So in 1979 the latch-side rule did not yet exist in the national standard we could read. What
  the 1961 (reaffirmed 1971) ANSI A117.1 said about room signs: **unverified** (text not found).
  Whether 1979 room numbers were on the door or on the wall: **unverified**; it needs a period
  photo of a university corridor.
- **Material.** Two-layer engraving laminate (engraved through the top colour to show the core)
  goes back to 1927 in the UK (Traffolyte) [C7, search]. That it was the usual US university
  room sign in 1979: **unverified**.

## 3. Exit signs

- **Lettering and size.** OSHA's exit sign rule (29 CFR 1910.37(b)(7)) was adopted from NFPA
  101-1970: letters of "Exit" at least 6 in high, principal strokes at least 3/4 in wide; a 1973
  change let existing externally lit signs with 4-1/2 in letters stay, except in places of
  assembly [C8, opened]. The 1976 edition's own text: **unverified** (not found).
- **Colour.** Neither NFPA nor OSHA fixed a colour, only a "distinctive color" [C9, search]. In
  the US red is the tradition; today Chicago and all of Illinois require red, and a few cities
  require green [C10, opened]. A building-code forum post quoting NFPA commentary says the code
  once required green, and in 1949 the fire marshals asked for red to come back [C11, search;
  second hand]. Whether Illinois required red already in 1979: **unverified**, but red is the
  safe choice for Evanston.
- **Look.** Early signs: white glass with red EXIT over a single bulb; later a box with larger
  letters backlit by an incandescent lamp [C10, opened; no years given].
- **Running man.** Designed by Yukio Ota in 1979 for a Japanese competition [C12, opened];
  submitted to ISO in 1980 [C13, search]; first ISO fire safety sign standard ISO 6309, first
  edition August 1987, replaced by ISO 7010:2011 [C14, search]. In the US the International
  Building Code first allowed pictogram exit signs in its 2021 edition [C10, opened]. A US
  corridor of 1979 had the word EXIT, never the running man.

## 4. Portable fire extinguisher

- **Types in use.** UL stopped listing inverting types (soda-acid, foam) in 1969; OSHA's 1980
  rule ordered soldered or riveted copper/brass shells out of service by 1 January 1982 [C15,
  search]. So in 1979 old copper units could still hang in an older building, while new ones
  were stored-pressure types.
- **Stored-pressure water.** A 1972 General WS-900: 2.5 gallons, polished stainless steel, made
  in the USA [C16, search; the listing itself returned 403]. A Canadian 1970s 2-1/2 gallon pump
  tank was lacquered copper [C17, search]. Our stainless 2.5 gal water unit fits the period.
- **A 1970 WS-900 photographed whole** (brought by the owner): Yahoo! Flea Market listing
  https://paypayfleamarket.yahoo.co.jp/item/z636560884 (opened in the browser): 19 photos; "高さ：約62.2cm",
  "直径：約18.3cm", "満水時重量表記：24 1/2 LBS", "1970年製", label "Chicago / Culver City Calif.". The top
  in photos 5, 6, 8, 9: a cast chrome valve on a hex collar; a long curved squeeze lever with a turned-down
  thumb end over a longer curved carry handle; a ring pin on a chain; a fill valve on a chain; a round
  gauge with a chrome bezel, dark blue dial, red arcs and a "RANGE" wedge at 100 (0-200); the hose's brass
  coupling at the valve's side. Shape reference only: the listing forbids reposting its photos.
  Ours against it (9.10), the top: a box for the valve where a cast chrome body sits on a hex collar;
  flat bars for the lever and the carry handle where both are curved castings, the lever's thumb end
  turned down; a white gauge face in a red rim facing forward where the dial is dark blue with red
  arcs and a RANGE wedge in a chrome bezel, facing the side; no ring pin, fill valve or their chains;
  no brass coupling where the hose leaves the valve; the body's seam band under the dome to check.
  All of the next list is fixed in src/app/lobby/extinguisher.js and extinguisher-label.js (10.10, each
  checked against the photos named), save the hanger and the back's vertical weld (it faces the wall);
  read off photos 5 and 8 besides: a small cream pointer at the dial's rim upper left and a cream tab
  across the red band at the bottom, both drawn.
  The redrawn one against all 19 photos (practice reviewer, 10.10), still to fix: the nozzle is a short
  brass tip held in a square stainless clip on the shell's left side, about 6 cm above the bottom (3, 10,
  14, 18, 19; the black slotted part in 15, 17 is the siphon tube inside); the dial's red band is narrow and
  runs from the RANGE wedge round the bottom to the other side, the wedge pale cream, the hub brass, no
  black needle showing, the dial set back inside a raised chrome lip (5, 8); ring pin and fill valve on the
  face opposite the gauge (2, 6, 9, 19), the pin through the valve's cast lugs about 10 mm from the axis

  extinguisher-flat-hook-bracket-ame01007-oo; chart https://www.amerex-fire.com/upl/downloads/library/
  bracket-reference.pdf); no source names a fork for a water unit.
  Amerex sells a fork too, also "for collar support", but for aluminium-valve cylinders of 1 to 10 lb

## 7. Paper and printed notices

- **Paper size.** Letter 8.5 × 11 in was the US size; the federal government used its own
  8 × 10.5 in "Government Letter" until the Reagan administration made Letter the norm for
  federal forms in the early 1980s [C21, opened]. A university notice of 1979 is Letter, not A4.
- **Typewriter.** The IBM Selectric II (1971) had dual pitch, 10 and 12 characters per inch;
  common elements: Courier 72 (10 pitch), Prestige Elite 72 (12 pitch), Letter Gothic [C22,
  search]. Courier: Howard Kettler, IBM, 1955 or 1956; Prestige Elite: Clayton Smith, IBM,
  1953 [C22, search]. A typed notice of 1979 is in one of these monospaced faces.
- **Copies.** Spirit duplicator ("ditto"): purple aniline dye copies, solvent smell; used in
  schools through the early 1980s, but photocopiers displaced it from the late 1960s through the
  1970s [C23, search]. So in 1979 a notice is a typed original, a photocopy, or a ditto copy.
  Mimeograph (stencil and black ink) is a different process [C23, search].
- **"Subjects needed" notices.** A Michigan Daily classified (undated page; other ads mention
  1967 and 1972 cars) reads "SUBJECTS NEEDED for psychology... $2.50/hr" [C24, search; page
  returned 403]. Tear-off tab flyers in 1970s psychology departments: **unverified** (no period
  photo found).

## 8. Corridor surfaces and lighting

- **Floor tile.** 9 in tile is the older size; Armstrong's 12 in vinyl-asbestos is dated to
  about 1960, and its Excelon line is reported as 12 in only from 1973 to 1980; EPA rules on
  asbestos came in 1980 [C25, search; forum and trade sources]. 12 × 12 in vinyl (asbestos)
  tile is right for 1979; 9 × 9 would read as an older (pre-1960s) floor.
- **Cove base, painted block colours, ceiling tile pattern, 1970s troffers:** no period source
  found; **unverified**. (Our F40T12 lamp and the 1975 federal light levels are already
  period sources in `building-standards.md`.)
