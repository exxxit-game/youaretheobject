## Findings, part B (Russian-language science)

### B7/B2 Yandex Toloka as a pool for experiments (Chapkovski 2023), full text read, saved objekt-papers/chapkovski-2023.txt
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9890829/ (J. Behavioral and Experimental Finance 37:100790,
doi:10.1016/j.jbef.2023.100790; University of Bonn; data Nov-Dec 2021, before Feb 2022)
- Pool: Russian speakers about 60% of active users, on average 9,028 online at any moment (range 2,927-13,667); by IP
  about 47% in Russia. Survey of 1,000 (990 complete): 58% male, 43% higher education, 81% in Russia.
- Speed: all Toloka slots filled within about 100 s; mean arrival 50 s vs 1,143 s on MTurk; under 75% of MTurk slots
  filled after 20 minutes.
- Pay: $0.10 per survey (MTurk $0.20, Prolific GBP 0.23, scaled to purchasing power); mean reported hourly income $1.78;
  about 31% call Toloka their main job; median 15 hours a week.
- Naivety (self-report only): about 14% took no surveys, about 60% twice a month or less.
- Benchmarks matched national polls: vaccination 42.7% vs Levada 46%; "cautious" generalized trust 78% vs World
  Values Survey 74%.
- Interactive games, few drop-outs: public goods 117 joined, 92 completed, none mid-game; rock-paper-scissors 111
  joined, 102 completed (vs about 18% drop-out on MTurk in Arechar et al. 2018).
- Honesty task: 65% reported heads in a private coin flip (honest = 50%), similar in Moscow, Voronezh, Arkhangelsk;
  71% for Russians in Hugh-Jones 2016; players expected 82%.
- Limits, in the author's words: "the platform lacks a clear and transparent procedure to filter out participants
  with low-quality submissions"; "there is no blocking option for researchers to filter out participants" from
  earlier similar studies; targeting needs "prescreening studies".
- Not in the paper: attention-check pass rates, bots, effect-size or reliability comparisons (not found).

### B1 A typical Russian VR experiment: MSU "Virtual Reality" centre (Menshikova, Savelyeva, Kovyazina 2018), full text saved objekt-papers/menshikova-2018.txt
https://cyberleninka.ru/article/n/otsenka-uspeshnosti-vosproizvedeniya-egotsentricheskih-i-allotsentricheskih-prostranstvennyh-reprezentatsiy-pri-ispolzovanii
(National Psychological Journal 2018 No. 2(30), pp. 113-122, doi:10.11621/npj.2018.0212; read via WebFetch)
- 36 adults (18 women, 18 men), mean age 23 +/- 2; a CAVE room (Barco iSpace, four 2.5 x 2.5 m screens, shutter
  glasses, eight IR tracking cameras), not a headset; training first; session about 16-20 min.
- Task: remember 7 objects in a 3D scene shown 25 s, then rebuild it from the front, left or above. Objects recalled
  about 6-7 of 7 from every view; layout accuracy front 0.90, left 0.79, above 0.68.
- Their stated reason for VR: "high ecological validity", "the possibility of presenting three-dimensional scenes",
  "the possibility of manipulating virtual objects" (our translation). No cybersickness or drop-out reported.
- Russian journal sites psyjournals.ru (Experimental Psychology (Russia), Psychological Science and Education) and
  soc4m.ru refused connection from here (region lock likely); CyberLeninka opens.
- Other Russian VR lines seen only in search snippets (unverified): Selivanov's VR-prompt studies of thinking
  (Experimental Psychology (Russia) 2017, 2019); a 2022 study comparing Vive headsets with monitors on mental states
  and creativity (Torrance, Rorschach); VRight avatar conversations for older adults (Clinical and Special
  Psychology 2021); a 2012 eye-head-body VR study (Experimental Psychology 5(3)).
- Pattern: Russian VR psychology is lab-based, small samples (tens), CAVE or tethered headsets, students and young
  adults; no home or standalone-headset study at scale found.

### B7/B4 Anketolog (Russian online panel): its own data-quality page and guidance
https://iom.anketolog.ru/kachestvo-dannyh (read via WebFetch)
- Studies of 1,600-6,000 people with a margin of error that "did not exceed 2.5%" (our translation).
- Checks: "the system verifies the mobile phone number of each new respondent"; staff "from time to time manually
  check" profile data against answers; on a mismatch "the respondent's answer is discarded ... and the respondent is
  blocked"; "we insert check questions into questionnaires to track the truthfulness and logic of the respondent's
  actions" (our translations).
- Not on the page: pay, speeders, straight-lining, bots, survey-frequency limits, rejection rates (not found).
https://iom.anketolog.ru/2021/03/11/kak-ponyat-iskrenni-li-vashi-respondenty (Anketolog's Institute of Public Opinion,
10 Mar 2021, read via WebFetch)
- Advice: "at least 3-4" control questions, placed "3-5 questions after the main one"; "not fewer than three duplicate
  questions"; trap questions about things that do not exist (our translations).
- Two Russian trap results it reports (second-hand; originals not read): in V. Yadov's study a non-existent book was
  "read" by "10% of respondents"; in an Institute of Sociology survey a made-up movement ("kukhteristy") won support
  from "1.2%" and opposition from "12.8%" (our translations).
- Testograf: no data-quality page read (not reached).

### B6 How Russian participants respond (seriousness, honesty, suspicion)
- Honesty lean in a private task (Toloka, read): 65% "heads" where honest reporting gives 50%; 71% in the earlier
  Russian population study cited there (Hugh-Jones 2016, not read).
- Caution toward others (Toloka, read): 78% "cautious" generalized trust, close to the World Values Survey's 74%.
- Answering about things that do not exist (Anketolog page, second-hand): 10% "read" a non-existent book; 1.2% backed
  and 12.8% opposed a made-up movement.
- Suspicion of deception: the elementy.ru item (opened by the owner) reports FeldmanHall, Dunsmoor, Tompary, Hunter,
  Todorov & Phelps (2018), "Stimulus generalization as a mechanism for learning to trust", PNAS, E1690-E1697: a US
  trust game where every partner was a program set to a fixed honesty (93%, 60%, 7% returns); questionnaires showed
  that "of 91 participants only five doubted" the partners were real, and their data were not used (our
  translation). Participants were promised a later turn as the partner, which never came, to keep the cover. Its
  figures (sent by the owner): money trusted in stage 1, $5.47 / $4.42 / $1.17 to the honest, neutral and dishonest
  partner over 15 trials; then new faces morphed 23-78% toward each. Not a Russian sample.
- Antisocial punishment (Herrmann, Thoni & Gachter 2008, Science 319:1362-1367, doi:10.1126/science.1153808; record
  read at https://www.alexandria.unisg.ch/43857): 16 pools; punishing cooperators is predicted by weak norms of civic
  cooperation and weak rule of law. Samara (Russia) and Minsk results: full text not read (unverified).
- A Russian study measuring participants' suspicion or seriousness in psychology experiments: not found (2 searches).

## C. What to copy, ranked (by how directly a source shows it changes data or player behaviour)
1. Record what the player does, ask afterwards, never "was it clear?": Valve ("what people do - not what they say",
   "People don't know why they do what they do"); Hodent (players say goals are clear, then cannot explain them). Our
   head and hand logs are the primary measure; the end questions only explain.
2. One short block of questions after the measured part, inside the headset, about six items: Schell ("only ...
   when they're done playing"); Bad News (6 items, longer would cut play). Survey before any open question (Valve order).
3. A control item that should not change, and one repeated question: Bad News (real-news items caught demand; d 0.03 vs
   0.30-0.36); Valve ("Validate responses (repetitive questions)"); Anketolog (3-4 control items, 3 duplicates).
4. Consent a few minutes into the game, plain words, withdraw any time, no IP or identity kept, session ID for
   duplicates: Bad News (43,687 consented, 14,266 completed pairs; GDPR); Remedy ("You can withdraw ... at any point").
5. Host timing: a ~2 s beat after an event before speaking, short pieces, silent while the eyes are needed, no scolding
   early: Portal 2 commentary. At most three new things per step, taught when the hand can do them: Hodent.
6. New-to-VR players: start calm, ask about discomfort every run, remove button abstractions: Meta guide, Schell,
   Owlchemy.
7. Small pilot playtests: no averages under 20 players, report "some/most", one fail in five matters: Meta guide.
8. Repeat players must be flagged by us: Toloka gives researchers no way to block past participants (Chapkovski);
   a per-device "already played this room" flag is our inference from that gap.
9. Russian online samples: plan exclusions in advance for inattention (Toloka 36% passing, unverified) and honesty
   lean (65% heads); one check about a thing that does not exist catches about 1 in 10 (second-hand).
10. Credibility: a named science adviser and people with lived experience (Hellblade), an ethics approval number
    (Bad News). Russian VR labs work in CAVEs with tens of students (MSU 2018); home headsets at scale would be new
    there - a reason a Russian lab might partner.

## Added: Druzhinin on the participant (the owner opened the page and sent its text)

V. N. Druzhinin, "Experimental Psychology" (textbook), sections 3.3-3.4, "The participant: his activity in the
experiment", as posted on https://www.psychology-online.net/articles/doc-10.html (region block for our tools; all
quotes our translation):
- Understanding the task is checked by "a short preliminary training series": success in it "serves as the
  criterion of understanding the instruction"; after the series, an interview to find difficulties and whether the
  participant swapped the experimenter's task for "his own subjective one". (The same practice step and closing
  question as the room recipe in objekt-files/notes/archive/docs/audit/process-rebuild.md.)
- "In any experiment there is an element of play, as if of work 'for pretend'", but it is also "a game in
  earnest": the owner's fun-and-serious, in a Russian textbook.
- Why volunteers come: in sensory-deprivation studies half came "out of curiosity alone"; often "to learn something
  about oneself"; money, course credit, friends; "extremely rarely" to serve science. About 7% of those recruited
  are volunteers (US data he cites); 70-90% of studies used college students.
- Volunteers differ (Rosenthal, "The Volunteer Subject"): more education, higher status and intelligence, more
  need for approval, more sociable. A "spoiled participant" who knows the scheme is avoided; naive ones preferred.
- Anonymous participants are more open, but named ones treat the task more responsibly.
- The experimenter's style changes results: in his twin study (WISC, teenagers 13-15, Rostov-on-Don) emotional
  support raised performance; control raised it too but weakened support's effect; twin correlation r = 0.82 in
  the neutral condition vs 0.36 under support without control. For us: the host's style is a variable to keep the
  same for every player.
- He recommends describing the experimental situation and its social factors in the publication.
