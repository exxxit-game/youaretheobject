## Part 1. Re-Search: recriando experimentos da Psicologia em videogame

Neves Filho, H. B., Farias, M. R. P., Couto, M. L. B. S., Eiterer, P. & Knaus, Y. C. (orgs.) (2020). Imagine
Publicacoes, Fortaleza. ISBN 978-65-991133-0-7. Free e-book; "Reproducao parcial ou integral permitida, bastando
apenas citar a fonte" (partial or full reproduction allowed, citing the source; our translation). Behaviour-analytic
(Skinnerian) school; authors mostly undergraduates at PUC-Goias and UFG (Goiania). 15 chapters: 1-2 frame the
project, 3-12 are ten recreations in Portal 2's built-in Puzzle Maker, 13-15 are method and context essays.

### 1.1 Aims and method, as Chapter 1 states them (Neves Filho & Knaus, pp. 3-13)
- Two aims: "ilustrar esses experimentos para fins didaticos, bem como construir maneiras de ampliar esses
  experimentos para testar outras variaveis" (to illustrate these experiments for teaching, and to build ways to
  extend them to test other variables; our translation), p. 3.
- The player takes the animal's place: "Em nossas recriacoes, um jogador assume o papel desses animais" (in our
  recreations a player takes the role of these animals; our translation), p. 4.
- The fidelity rule: each group rebuilt the original setting "se pautando pela similaridade funcional, se nao,
  quando possivel, fisica" (guided by functional similarity, and where possible physical; our translation), p. 7.
  The same measure is kept (Thorndike timed escapes, so the recreation times escapes).
- Origin: a short summer course on History of Psychology and Experimental Design, PUC-Goias, first semester 2017.
  Four steps: lectures on 10 classic experiments (independent/dependent variables stressed); one group per
  experiment, tutored into experts; each group "recriou funcionalmente as contingencias" in Portal 2; brief data
  collection and a report (each report is a chapter). Five Steam accounts for ten groups; remote tutoring.
- Outcome stated frankly: "Alguns dos nossos procedimentos replicaram os achados originais, outros nao" (some of
  our procedures replicated the original findings, others did not; our translation), p. 7. Failed recreations are
  framed as useful for methodological discussion.
- Why Portal 2: first person, a point-and-click map maker needing no programming, cheap (a little over R$30), runs
  on old PCs, maps shareable on Steam with descriptions (science outreach; a growing "pool" of maps refined by
  later groups). Alternative to costly rat teaching labs; fast for student projects that would otherwise wait for
  apparatus, animals and ethics committees.
- Data protocol: printed controls sheet beside the PC; screen-only recording with OBS "de modo a manter o
  participante anonimo" (to keep the participant anonymous; our translation), p. 10; hand coding of video, with
  BORIS or JWatcher suggested.
- Ethics in practice: most data chapters say participants signed a free and informed consent form; no chapter
  reports ethics-committee approval. Participants were mostly students recruited verbally; samples 3 to 19.

### 1.2 Chapter by chapter
Format: original; what stands for what; data; faithfulness. "Map maker" = Portal 2 Puzzle Maker.

**Ch 2. The digital game as contingency** (Farias & Heck, pp. 14-28). No experiment. Argues a game is a contingency
in behaviour-analytic terms and that "o estimulo virtual e, em todos os seus parametros, real para o individuo"
(the virtual stimulus is in every parameter real for the individual; our translation), p. 20. Uses Suits (a game
as a voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles) and McGonigal's four parts (goal, rules, feedback system,
voluntary participation). Game design as a tool against monotonous lab tasks (cites Oliveira 1998, whose earlier
participants complained tasks were monotonous); game telemetry as a data source.

**Ch 3. Thorndike's law of effect and learning curve** (Franco, A. V. Santos, A. R. R. Silva, Sousa, Abdala Filho,
Lima, Leao, Pereira & Neves Filho, pp. 29-40). Original: Thorndike (1898), puzzle boxes. Mapping: room = puzzle box,
player = cat; 20 ball-operated floor buttons scattered at random, one ball, a laser barrier before the exit; only
button 14 switches off the laser (= the cat's string or pedal); the other 19 buttons, walking, jumping, shooting =
the cat's ineffective clawing and squeezing; exit = escape. Measure kept: time to escape, 5 exposures. Data: 3 women,
18-29, non-psychology students, one-way-mirror room, consent signed; instruction: leave the room, then repeat 4 more
times. Escape times 143 to 28 s, 285 to 58 s, 350 to 32 s, flat over trials 3-5; wrong-button placements fell.
Faithfulness: "um ambiente similar, em questoes funcionais" (functionally similar; our translation); results said to
resemble the original. No food or deprivation; escape is the only consequence.

**Ch 4. "Insight" (title: Kohler 1917) broken into prerequisite skills** (Moraes, Bovo, Guimaraes, Lira, Farias,
Franca Filho & Pires, pp. 41-58). Despite the title, the procedure recreates Epstein, Kirshnit, Lanza & Rubin (1984),
pigeons who combined separately trained "push box to target" and "climb box, peck banana". Mapping: portal-training
room (cross gaps with portals over deadly water, at least 5 crossings) and box-training room (stack 3 boxes into
stairs to the exit) = the two trained repertoires; test room where boxes fall endlessly onto unreachable platforms
and must be brought down through a ceiling portal and stacked = the novel combination. Data: 6 players with
first-person-game but no Portal experience; 3 trained (boxes-first or portals-first), 3 test only. All trained solved
(7.6, 6.2, 22.7 min); of the untrained, one solved in 6 min (credited to past shooter games), two quit (28 and
16.9 min). Faithfulness: aimed to recreate Epstein "da maneira mais precisa possivel" (as precisely as possible; our
translation), p. 44; concludes it corroborates Epstein. The conclusion first says untrained players did not solve,
then notes the exception.

**Ch 5. Lashley's (conditional) discrimination** (Sousa, Leao, Prado, Pires, Abdalla Filho, Franca Filho, Carvalho
& Pereira, pp. 59-67). Original: Lashley (1930), rats jumping from a stand at one of two cards. Mapping: jump into
one of two pits under two wall symbols = the jump; one pit leads to the exit (= food), the other to a turret that
kills the avatar, respawning it (= the punished choice). Symbols square/circle and box/ball; four conditions of 6
trials, side alternated; 5 min control practice first. Data: 3 women; first choices correct 67, 61, 78, 89 % by
condition. Faithfulness: "nao foi possivel replicar os achados originais de Lashley" (it was not possible to
replicate Lashley's findings; our translation), p. 66. Cause named: 6 trials per condition against Lashley's 128,
260 and 350-700 per pair; a contextual cue signalling the contingency is suggested.

**Ch 6. Tolman's cognitive maps and latent learning** (Pereira, A. V. Santos, Camacho, Ferreira & Selhorst,
pp. 68-75). Original: Tolman & Honzik (1930). Mapping: one maze room; group 1 explores 5 min with the exit door
removed (= unrewarded trials), then the door is put back; group 2 goes straight to the maze with exit (10 min cap).
Data: 6 adults, 3 per group: all three explorers exited (2:52, 6:18, 2:28); one control exited (2:07, a gamer who had
played Portal 2), two did not. Faithfulness: aims to "replicar sistematicamente" (replicate systematically); data
called similar to Tolman's. Unflagged gaps: the discussion says 10 minutes of prior exposure where the method says 5;
one exposure instead of Tolman's daily runs; the one control who exited had played Portal 2 (they note it).

**Ch 7. Maier's reasoning: integrating separately learned parts** (Bovo, Guimaraes, Ribeiro, Lira, Couto, Eiterer &
Neves Filho, pp. 76-89). Original: Maier (1931), rats combining table exploration with a separately learned raised
path to food. Mapping: exploration room viewed through glass with "only look, do not interact" (= experience 1);
five-floor room with button-started timed elevators (= experience 2); test room combining cube-activated light
bridges and elevator buttons (= the combined route). Failure = over 5 deaths or 10 min. Data: 11 adults (20-41),
split by first-person-game experience; phases varied by person. "Os resultados nao indicam uma replicacao direta"
(results do not indicate a direct replication; our translation), p. 84; game experience mattered most; partial
replication in inexperienced players. Faithfulness issues they list: Maier's rats were naive to the apparatus while
players had varied game histories; game bugs (flickering light bridge, cube falling at random); players ignored the
look-only rule and ran for the exit; impatience led to quitting. Fix suggested: an exit-less room with timed
exploration ended by the experimenter.

**Ch 8. Harlow's learning sets** (Couto & Eiterer, pp. 90-104). Original: Harlow (1949), Wisconsin General Test
Apparatus. Mapping: each trial is a small black room with two openings under two 3x3 white-tile drawings; walking
through an opening = displacing an object; correct = the experimenter drops a marble in a mug (tokens exchanged for
sweets) = food under the object. Conditions: object discrimination, reversal, position (side alternating each
problem); 5 problems x 3 or 4 trials. Data: 19 adults; groups by instruction (full, partial, none). Object and
reversal mostly good; position mediocre; the no-instruction group did poorly (two of three did not grasp that marbles
were the consequence). Faithfulness, stated outright: "Este experimento se configura enquanto recriacao e nao uma
replicacao sistematica, dado que nele foram utilizadas instrucoes" (this is a recreation, not a systematic
replication, since instructions were used; our translation), pp. 92-93. Harlow ran 2450 trials, they 45 or 60;
instructions are "um atalho" (a shortcut) for the learning history. Also: virtual tasks mean "os participantes nao se
submetem a riscos reais" (participants face no real risk; our translation), p. 104. Experience counts disagree
between method and results.

**Ch 9. Antonitis: response variability under reinforcement** (Pires, Franca Filho & Pereira, pp. 105-113).
Original: Antonitis (1951), rats nose-poking anywhere along a long slot, positions photographed, across operant
level, conditioning, extinction, reconditioning. Mapping: 9 push buttons side by side = slot positions; a second room
behind the start with 9 exit doors = food tray (moved there "devido a restricoes de programacao", owing to map-maker
limits; our translation); any press opens the doors in training; distinct sounds for opening or not. Data: 3 adults
with little game experience; summed presses: baseline 113, 67% at the outer buttons; training mostly central;
extinction spread out again with a peak at button 5. Claimed to corroborate Antonitis. Gaps: no repeated
extinction/reconditioning; habituation placed before baseline; their own training counts add to 17 presses with 12
central, not the "11 of 16" stated.

**Ch 10. Pryor, Haag & O'Reilly's creative porpoise** (Maia, Moraes, Aleixo, Sabino, Farias & Araujo Junior,
pp. 114-133). Original: Pryor et al. (1969), reinforcing only never-seen behaviour in a dolphin. Mapping: a room full
of interacting objects (cubes, sphere, gels, lasers, ramps, jump pad) = the dolphin's freedom of movement; the
experimenter joins as a second co-op player in a sealed observation booth (two PCs); marbles in a mug for each new
behaviour = the whistle and fish. A list of 21 pre-agreed behaviours was needed. Phases: operant level 5 min,
experimenter models 3 unseen behaviours, 15 min reinforced phase. Data: 5 players (4 knew Portal 2); a variability
pattern in 3 of 5. Faithfulness: "e impossivel dizer que houve uma replicacao sistematica" (it is impossible to say
there was a systematic replication; our translation), p. 132; "a liberdade de movimentos de Hou nao foi equiparada"
(Hou's freedom of movement was not matched; our translation), p. 133; untrained observers; rules from Portal habits.

**Ch 11. Weiss: forward versus backward chaining** (Campos & Farias, pp. 134-148). Original: Weiss (1978), students
learning 6-response sequences on a button box; forward chaining gave fewer errors; long written instructions.
Mapping: 4 rooms, each 4 halls x 4 floors; on each floor put the box on one of 4 floor buttons; a wrong button drops a
laser on the box (= error tone); wall gel colour per floor and lamp positions = the symbols; sequences of 4. The
engine cannot fire an event only after an ordered sequence, so the chain is built in space. Data: 8 adults, short vs
long instruction (the long one their manipulation, since Weiss's instruction could be a confound). Short rule: "nao
replicacao" (no replication; players treated it as one long sequence); long rule: forward chaining fewer errors,
as in Weiss.

**Ch 12. Epstein's resurgence** (A. R. R. Silva, Sousa, Pires, Abdala Filho, Lima, Leao, Pereira, Franca Filho &
Knaus, pp. 149-158). Original: Epstein (1983), pigeons. Mapping: R1 cube into socket, R2 press a button, R3 (control)
fire the portal gun; exit = grain. Phases: 3 min baseline, R1 opens the door (3 exits in a row), R2 opens it while R1
is extinguished, 5 min test with both extinguished. Data: 3 women. R1 exceeded R2 in the test for all three,
so "a replicacao foi bem-sucedida" (the replication succeeded; our translation), p. 157. Their caveat: R3 was
also high (cheap; its lights and sounds may reinforce it); use a cost-matched control. Unflagged: participant 1
made 7 R1 at baseline and 7 in the test, so no rise over baseline.

**Ch 13. Inferring behavioural events from Portal 2's own log** (Picanco & Silva Filho, pp. 159-170). Replaces video
coding with the engine's event log: `net_showevents 1` (uses, first sight of an entity, drops, portal shots and
entries, with ticks at 60 per second), `con_logfile`, demo recording and replay, a Python script to tabulate, and a
modified SourceAutoRecord plugin (`log_session`). Position and view angle are not logged; suggests periodic
`spec_pos` or extracting from demo replays after the fact; proposes syncing gaze, face, heart rate, skin conductance,
EEG. Logging is "complementar ao registro observacional, e nao ... um substituto" (complementary to observation, not
a substitute; our translation), p. 160.

**Ch 14. Games as a platform for data sharing and citizen science** (Santana, pp. 171-175). An open, multi-site
database of recreations; students as experimenters; the platform "pode permitir a coleta anonima de dados" (can allow
anonymous data collection; our translation), p. 173; fewer teaching animals; teach participants to read the raw data.

**Ch 15. Culture and video games** (Alberto Santos, pp. 176-188). History of arcades, consoles, art and e-sports in
Skinner's terms; no experiment, nothing for us beyond context.

### 1.3 What our game can take from the book (ranked)
1. Write down "what stands for what" for every room (original element, our element, why functionally equal), as
   every recreation chapter does; it doubles as the reveal's "how the original worked" panel.
2. Trial count is their most common cause of failure (Lashley 6 vs 128-700; Harlow 45-60 vs 2450; Antonitis cut
   phases). In a 10-minute room, prefer effects that show in a few trials or one test (Thorndike's curve in 5
   escapes, latent learning, resurgence) and say plainly when the dose is cut.
3. Prior game experience swamped the effect in chapters 4, 6, 7, 8 and 10. Teach the controls in a neutral practice
   space first (ch 5 used 5 min), ask about VR and game experience, and record it.
4. Instructions are an experimental variable (ch 8, 11): fix the wording, report it, change it only on purpose.
5. Keep the consequence inside the world and self-evident: the exit worked (ch 3, 9, 12); tokens handed over by
   a person were missed (ch 8). At home with no experimenter this matters more.
6. Make rules physical, not verbal: "look, do not touch" failed (ch 7); build the constraint into the room.
7. Log every event with timestamps and replay from the log (ch 13), not video coding; we also have head pose
   every frame, which Portal 2 lacked. This fits "replay your moment".
8. Pick control responses matched in effort and free of side effects (ch 12).
9. For free-form behaviour, fix the coded list in advance (ch 10).
10. Report failures and deviations openly (ch 1, 5, 8, 10): a replication that differs is still teachable.
Limits: samples of 3-19, no statistics, several arithmetic and procedure inconsistencies, no ethics approval
reported. Use it for method, never as evidence that an effect replicates in games.

## Part 2. Colombia, Ministry of Health, Resolution 8430 of 4 October 1993

Title: "normas cientificas, tecnicas y administrativas para la investigacion en salud" (scientific, technical and
administrative rules for health research; our translation). The preamble ties it to Law 10 of 1990: rules
"de obligatorio cumplimiento por las entidades que integran el Sistema" (binding on the entities of the National
Health System; our translation).
- **Art. 2 (committee):** "Las instituciones que vayan a realizar investigacion en humanos, deberan tener un Comite
  de Etica en Investigacion, encargado de resolver todos los asuntos relacionados con el tema" (institutions that
  will do research on humans must have a Research Ethics Committee to resolve all related matters; our
  translation). Art. 3: each such institution writes an internal procedures manual. Art. 6(g): research needs the
  institution's legal representative's authorization, the participants' informed consent and approval by the
  institution's committee.
- **Art. 4 (scope):** health research includes actions contributing "(a) Al conocimiento de los procesos biologicos
  y sicologicos en los seres humanos" (to knowledge of biological and psychological processes in humans; our
  translation). So a psychology experiment falls inside its definition.
- **Art. 5-6:** dignity and welfare prevail; research rests on prior animal, lab or other scientific work, runs
  only when the knowledge cannot be had otherwise, states its (minimal) risks, has written informed consent "con las
  excepciones dispuestas", and is run by qualified professionals under a health entity's responsibility.
  Art. 7 random assignment to groups; Art. 8 privacy (identify only when needed and authorized); Art. 9 risk = the
  probability of immediate or late harm; Art. 10 investigators must name the risk types.
- **Art. 11 (risk categories):**
  (a) without risk: retrospective documentary studies "y aquellos en los que no se realiza ninguna intervencion o
  modificacion intencionada de las variables biologicas, fisiologicas, sicologicas o sociales" (and those with no
  intentional intervention or change in biological, physiological, psychological or social variables; our
  translation), e.g. record reviews, interviews, questionnaires that neither identify people nor touch sensitive
  conduct.
  (b) minimal risk: prospective studies with common procedures, listed "entre los que se consideran" (among which)
  weighing, ECG, hearing tests, venepuncture within limits, moderate exercise in healthy volunteers, "pruebas
  sicologicas a grupos o individuos en los que no se manipulara la conducta del sujeto" (psychological tests of
  groups or individuals in which the subject's behaviour will not be manipulated; our translation).
  (c) greater than minimal: probability of affecting the subject is significant (radiology, new drugs or devices,
  surgery, placebo-controlled treatment, and so on).
- **Art. 12:** the principal investigator stops at once on any risk to health, and for any subject who asks to stop.
- **Art. 14-15 (consent):** consent is a written agreement given with full knowledge, free choice and no coercion;
  it must explain purpose, procedures (marking experimental ones), discomforts and risks, benefits, alternatives,
  answers to questions, freedom to withdraw at any time without prejudice, non-identification and confidentiality,
  updated information during the study, care and compensation for harm, and who pays extra costs.
- **Art. 16 (valid consent):** written by the principal investigator, reviewed by the committee, names and
  addresses of two witnesses, signed by both witnesses and the subject (fingerprint if he cannot sign), made in
  duplicate. **Paragraph 1 (waivers):** "En el caso de investigaciones con riesgo minimo, el Comite ... por razones
  justificadas, podra autorizar que el Consentimiento Informado se obtenga sin formularse por escrito y tratandose de
  investigaciones sin riesgo, podra dispensar al investigador de la obtencion del mismo" (for minimal-risk research
  the committee may, for justified reasons, allow consent not in writing; for research without risk it may excuse
  the investigator from obtaining it; our translation). Paragraph 2: if the subject depends on the investigator,
  someone independent obtains consent. Paragraphs 3-5: capacity checks by a neurologist, psychiatrist or
  psychologist.
- **Other relevant articles:** Art. 19 participation "enteramente voluntaria" in community research; Art. 25-26
  minors need guardian consent plus a neurologist's, psychiatrist's or psychologist's certificate of capacity, and
  the minor's own assent; Art. 45-46 students are a "subordinate group": refusal must not affect their standing and
  the committee must include a member of that group; Art. 87(a) animal research should use mathematical models and
  computer simulation where appropriate, Art. 89 the 3R principle.
- **What this means for us (reading of the text, not legal advice):** (1) By Art. 11(a) a room that deliberately
  manipulates behaviour is not "without risk", so the full consent waiver of Art. 16 par. 1 does not fit by the
  letter. (2) Art. 11(b)'s psychological-test example excludes behaviour manipulation, but its list is open-ended,
  so the committee classifies; if it rules minimal risk, consent may be unwritten (a click-through) with its
  authorization. (3) Who must have a committee: "institutions" doing research on humans, within the National
  Health System; whether a foreign independent game studio is such an institution is not settled by the text and
  needs a Colombian legal reading. (4) Minors: the Art. 25 capacity certificate is impractical at home, a reason to
  keep minors out in Colombia.

## Part 3. Van den Bussche, Verhaegen, Reynvoet & Hughes (2026), Citizen science in psychology

Behavior Research Methods, doi:10.3758/s13428-026-03019-8. The file is the accepted manuscript from the University
of Essex repository (repository.essex.ac.uk/43107), so pages are manuscript pages; literature counts as of 17
December 2025. Its licence is not stated in the file, so it is paraphrased here with one short quote.
- **Position:** they model citizen science (CS) on two axes, citizens' activity level and involvement in design and
  planning (the "2D-CS" model). In psychology, "mere participation in data collection would typically not be
  considered as CS" (p. 18): citizens must take an active role in some research step. Large open-entry online
  crowdsourcing studies are allowed as low-level CS in the bottom-left corner. Only 5.9% of CS publications have a
  psychology subfield as main Web of Science category.
- **Studies it lists.** Data-giving online (low level): BBC Internet study (Reimers 2007); implicit-attitudes
  demonstration site (Nosek et al. 2002); Web Experimental Psychology Lab (Reips 2001); LabintheWild (Reinecke &
  Gajos 2015); Children Helping Science; The Great Brain Experiment smartphone games (Brown et al. 2014); Sea Hero
  Quest, navigation in millions of people (Spiers et al. 2023). High activity: Booth et al. 2020 (UK, over 7000
  pupils, physical-activity breaks and cognition, teachers ran it, BBC Terrific Scientific); Szigeti et al. 2021
  (self-blinded psychedelic microdosing; benefits explained by placebo). Co-created: Gignac et al. 2022 (Barcelona
  residents co-designed a study of NO2 and cognitive control); Letang et al. 2021 (teachers co-designed and ran a
  randomized inhibitory-control training). Other psychology: mental health (Todowede et al. 2023 review of nine CS
  studies; Bonhoure et al. 2023 social-dilemma experiments co-created with a mental-health community; Winther &
  Jorgensen 2023), neurodiversity (Fletcher-Watson et al. 2019; den Houting et al. 2020; Sonuga-Barke et al.
  2024), parent-led Parenting Science Gang (Collins et al. 2020).
- **Data quality:** criticised for cheap tools, loose protocols, citizen bias. Remedies: random checks against an
  expert, training and clear protocols, comparing with standard measurements (Snik et al. 2014 found good
  agreement), crowd aggregation and cognitive modelling. Cognitive tasks need precise timing and control, hard
  outside the lab.
- **Recruitment and diversity:** finding and keeping volunteers is hard; drop-out is common; CS samples skew by
  gender, ethnicity and income (Pateman et al. 2021). Remedies: publish participant demographics, targeted personal
  invitations (Brouwer & Hessels 2019), several ways to take part.
- **Researcher-subject paradox:** a participant who also collects data may know the hypothesis (social
  desirability, confirmation bias). Remedies: separate collectors from subjects, self-blinding, or involve citizens
  in questions, design and interpretation, where the paradox does not arise. Co-defining questions (high
  involvement, low effort) is called especially accessible for psychology.
- **Ethics and consent:** APA code (2017), Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and GDPR still apply; citizens who handle
  others' data get compulsory ethics training; tested citizens get an extended consent saying who collects their data
  and how CS changes data handling; a data-management plan names who can access and store data (Resnik 2019).
- **Feedback to participants:** Booth et al. gave each child a summary of their own results and teachers the
  anonymised group results with class materials. Passive participation can feel meaningless and alienating
  (Malmqvist 2019); telling participants the study's goals and likely outcomes counters it (Schonfeld & Rasmussen
  2019). Recommendation 8: explain goals, methods and implications to build trust.
- **Their eight recommendations:** use CS organisations' resources (EU-Citizen.Science, ECSA Ten Principles, UCL
  course, CitizenScience.gov toolkit); learn from earlier CS projects in the field; decide deliberately what citizens
  do (not every step); get help with communication and project management; address the researcher-subject paradox;
  co-create research questions; handle ethics and law (training, adapted consent, GDPR, data plan); share
  practices and start small.
- **What our game should take:** (1) Our loop (be the subject, then see how you were caught and what the study
  found) is the remedy they name for passive participation; give each player his own result beside the group's and
  the original's, as Booth did. (2) The paradox backs our order: the room first and naive, the hypothesis only in
  the reveal. (3) By their definition a game that only collects play data is not CS in psychology; do not use the
  label unless players get an active role, the cheapest being a vote on which question or room comes next. (4) Home
  play lacks lab control: put the protocol in code, log timing, compare our numbers with the original paper's, rely
  on large samples. (5) Consent must say plainly what is recorded, who keeps it and for how long; keep a written data
  plan. (6) Record minimal demographics to report how skewed our sample is (headset owners are not the public).
