### 1d/5. Spain: SPALEX, a Spanish word-knowledge test with 209,351 players - page read
- Aguasvivas, Carreiras, Brysbaert, Mandera, Keuleers & Dunabeitia (2018), "SPALEX: A Spanish Lexical Decision
  Database From a Massive Online Data Collection", Frontiers in Psychology 9:2156.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6240651/
  - Who: Basque Center on Brain, Language and Cognition (BCBL, San Sebastian), Ghent, Tilburg, Nebrija.
  - Reach: up to 19 December 2017, "209,351 participants completed 282,576 tests"; native speakers only:
    169,628 people, 227,655 sessions. "68.88%" of the data came in the first month, when "an advertising
    campaign was done in order to attract the public's attention"; players could share results on social networks.
  - Built-in cheat check: each session 70 words and 30 nonwords; players were told some strings were nonce words
    "for which they would be penalized" if marked as known.
  - Cleaning: non-natives out (17.44% of data); response times kept only within 200-2,000 ms; outliers beyond 1.5
    box lengths removed.
  - Ethics: "BCBL Ethics Committee" (a research centre's own committee); consent by button click; "no personal
    information that could identify any participant was collected".
- Same design in Flemish/English (Ghent): the English vocabulary test tested "221,268 individuals who returned
  265,346 sessions"; 67 words and 33 non-words, players "told they would be punished for yes-responses" to
  non-words; score = yes to words minus yes to non-words; only "the first three sessions" kept per repeat player;
  "At no point they were asked to identify themselves"; review under "the General Ethical Protocol" of Ghent's
  psychology faculty. Brysbaert, Stevens, Mandera & Keuleers (2016), Frontiers in Psychology 7:1116,
  https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01116/full . The Dutch original (Keuleers et al. 2015,
  QJEP 68:1665-1692, "nearly 300,000" participants) was seen only in search listings (unverified).
- Lesson for us: a plain task with a built-in honesty check (fake items that cost points) and a score to share
  reached hundreds of thousands with no pay; one media burst gave two thirds of all data.

### 4/1h. Israel: hospital Helsinki committees also review psychology, and take companies for a fee
- Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center, Helsinki committee page. https://www.tasmc.org.il/rd/next-unit/helsinki-committee/
  - Scope: besides clinical trials it "also reviews research proposals in the social field (psychology, social
    medicine and requests that include questionnaires)" (our translation of the Hebrew).
  - Companies: for company-initiated studies "the company must pay handling fees on submission" (our
    translation); new application 7,500 NIS; extension or new protocol version 1,000 NIS; consent-form changes free.
  - Follows the Ministry of Health procedure and ICH-GCP; decision time not stated (meetings by calendar).
  - Whether a company-initiated, non-medical psychology game would be accepted there: not stated (unverified).
- Israeli online panels: iPanel's answers to the ESOMAR 28 questions (the owner brought the PDF): "more than 100,000
  active members"; points per survey by length, redeemed for gift cards; invited to "no more than 4-5 surveys each
  month", answering "2-3"; each data file tested on open and closed answers, "inconsistencies and response time";
  inconsistencies tracked across surveys, "repeat offenders are then removed"; age and gender checked at entry; an ID
  copy to redeem points; certified to no quality system. ESOMAR's 28 questions are the market-research checklist
  for buying an online sample: the questions to ask any panel we use.
  No Israeli home-VR or game-based psychology study was found in two searches (not found).

### 3. Germany: a longer test at home makes people dodge the test, not quit (NEPS experiment) - abstract read
- Kleinert, Christoph & Ruland (2021), "Experimental evidence on immediate and long-term consequence of test-induced
  respondent burden for panel attrition", Sociological Methods & Research 50(4):1552-1583 (IAB record).
  https://iab.de/en/publications/publication/?id=5898751
  - Design: German National Educational Panel Study adults, "experimental split questionnaire design administering
    two tests to one half of the respondents".
  - Result: the longer variant "had no higher probability of refusing to participate in the survey" but they were
    "significantly more likely to apply alternative strategies for not taking the tests"; and "Not only test
    avoidance but also low test performance has negative consequences for survey participation in later waves."
  - Lesson for us (our reading): at home, burden shows up as quiet non-effort inside the task rather than as
    drop-out - so effort must be measured inside the measured moment, and the room kept short.
- Humour itself: no peer-reviewed European experiment manipulating humour in instructions was found (one search;
  US market-research items only: not used).

### 3/5. Netherlands: Hooked (Amsterdam), points that reward speed, plus a check that the claim is true
- Burgoyne, Bountouridis, Van Balen & Honing (2013), "Hooked: A game for discovering what makes music catchy", ISMIR
  2013, Music Cognition Group, University of Amsterdam, and Utrecht University (text read via pdftotext).
  https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000101.pdf ; text saved objekt-papers/burgoyne-2013.txt
  - Game in the measured task: while players listen "points are counting down; the faster" they recognise the
    piece, the more points they win; each task designed "in what we felt was the most entertaining fashion
    possible" in order to "recruit the largest number" of players.
  - The honesty problem a game creates, named: "In a competitive game environment, it is not" (a fast claim is not
    proof of recognition); "We needed a task to verify that players have truly recognised the music at the moments
    they claim so": the sound mutes, players follow along in their heads, then judge whether playback resumed
    in the right place.
  - Pilot: "26 testers from within our academic networks", about 45 minutes each; abstract: "players are not as
    reliable as one might expect at predicting" their own recall.
  - Reach: a Manchester science-festival version (#Hooked, with the Museum of Science and Industry) is listed in
    search with "over 200,000 people" playing (undated page, not opened: unverified).
- Lesson for us: when the game rewards a fast answer, add a built-in check that the answer is real (as SPALEX does
  with fake words), instead of trusting the player's click.

### 1d. Italy, 1g. Poland and Czechia
- Italy: one Italian search found no home or game-based psychology study at scale. A University of Pisa
  open-source VR spider avoidance test: the owner opened the article (scientificult.it, 7 Oct 2026, from the
  University of Pisa press office; our translation). It replicates "a test used since the 1960s where people find
  themselves in a corridor at the end of which there is a big spider"; people were asked to approach it as close
  as possible, measuring completion time and walking speed; 75 participants with varying arachnophobia; avoidance
  subtypes read from approach trajectories. Paper: Frumento, Magnavacca, Iannizzotto, Gemignani, Scilingo,
  Menicucci & Greco (2026), Communications Psychology, doi:10.1038/s44271-026-00530-8; tool free on OSF,
  doi:10.17605/OSF.IO/YN29T. A published VR re-creation of a classic test with a corridor and an approach path;
  phobic content, so not for unsupervised home play without its own safeguards.
- Poland and Czechia: one Polish/English search found no online psychology experiment at the thousands scale
  (not found).

## 6. What these regions add that the English-language sources did not (drawn only from the entries above)
1. Public media and live events as the recruitment engine, not Reddit or paid panels: Danish state broadcaster DR
   brought "more than 16,000" unpaid players to Skill Lab; SPALEX got "68.88%" of 209,351 players in the month of
   its advertising campaign; Sigman's TEDxperiments ran on 5,180 people in one hall.
2. Honesty checks built into the game itself: Hooked's mute-and-verify step and the Ghent/SPALEX fake words that
   cost points - cheaper and less "lab-like" than attention-check questions.
3. A story or a host inside the measured task, at scale and published: Skill Lab's "detective narrative theme"
   (16,000 players, 72 minutes of plain tasks done unpaid), the scientist as stage host (Nature Human Behaviour).
4. The sideload cliff, measured: EventLab lost 39 of 65 people exactly at "download the application and upload it
   to their head-mounted display" - our browser WebXR start removes that step.
5. Burden at home shows as quiet test-dodging, not quitting (German NEPS experiment) - effort has to be checked
   inside the moment.
6. Law: in the Netherlands, Switzerland, Germany and (outside the medical "Jarde" scope) France, a non-medical
   psychology study has no legal review duty, yet journals and funders demand review; EUREC (2021) says such
   researchers may find no committee and points to medical committees. Paid doors open to companies exist:
   METC LDD non-WMO opinion 500 euros (NL), Sweden's state authority 5,000 SEK (any legal person; review is
   legally required there when a method aims to affect the mind), Tel Aviv hospital Helsinki committee 7,500 NIS
   (reviews psychology), private centre committees (CEMIC Argentina, BCBL Spain).
7. Brazil allows covert designs by rule: research without telling goals and procedures when telling would change
   the behaviour, consent afterwards; consent may be oral or electronic (CNS Res. 510/2016).
8. A stricter biometric reading: the Belgian regulator counts data as biometric when identification is merely
   possible "given the nature of the data" - raw head-and-hand traces would be GDPR art. 9 data; derived measures
   that cannot re-identify fall outside.
9. Cost and team: a CNRS/Glitchers review puts a custom science game at 50,000-300,000 (currency sign lost in the
   text) and blames failures on games "not built for science"; infrequent gamers give noisier data on complex tasks
   (cited study not read).
