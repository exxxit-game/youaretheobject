## Findings

### 2-JP. Japanese crowdsourcing as a participant pool: data quality vs students

- Majima, Nishiyama, Nishihara & Hata (2017), Frontiers in Psychology 8:378, "Conducting Online Behavioral Research
  Using Crowdsourcing Services in Japan" (Hokusei Gakuen University). Full text read:
  https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00378/full
  - Platform: CrowdWorks only. Pay: "The participants received 50 JPY for completing a 10 min survey" (Survey 1);
    80 JPY for a 15-min survey (Surveys 2 and 3).
  - N analysed: Survey 1 295 workers + 131 students; Survey 2 297 workers + 156 students; Survey 3 167 workers
    (81 mobile, 85 PC).
  - Attention (instructional manipulation check, IMC), Survey 2: students passed 35.3%, workers 53.6%; "more CW
    participants successfully passed the IMC than students"; "The failure rate of the present CW participants (46%)".
  - Small screens, Survey 3: IMC pass 69.1% on phones vs 92.9% on PC (odds ratio 0.22, 95% CI 0.06-0.83, p = 0.026);
    "small screen devices are found to impair participants' attention to instructions".
  - Ethics and consent: "approved and conducted in compliance with the guidelines of the Hokusei Gakuen University
    Ethics Committee"; "All of the participants gave their web-based informed consent instead of written consent."
  - Advice: IMC "could be a useful tool for the elimination of inattentive responses in online studies"; "ask workers
    to participate in online studies using relatively large screen devices"; check naivety (prior similar surveys,
    the CRT); "the research community may be called on to establish guidelines for ethically valid compensation".
  - For us: students in a lab failed the attention check MORE often than paid home workers (65% vs 46%); the device
    (small screen) mattered more than the place. A headset is a large screen, but the lesson is: measure attention
    per device, do not assume the lab is cleaner.
- Seaborn & Nakamura (2025), Frontiers in Psychology 10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1588579, "Quality and representativeness of
  research online with Yahoo! Crowdsourcing" (abstract read only so far:
  https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1588579/text):
  "65 data sets comprising N = 60, 681 participants"; "generally high completion (77.6%) and retention rates (70.1%)";
  "use of multimedia stimuli exhibited higher completion (97.7%) and retention (91.9%) rates"; the general setting
  "attracted middle-aged men". (Full text: see below if read.)
  - Full text read (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1588579/full):
    authors at Institute of Science Tokyo and Meiji University; platform "reportedly has 85 million users as of
    November 2024" (vendor figure); pay: "compensation as points through the cashless payment service PayPay".
    Definitions: completion = finished; retention = "data points that are preserved after checking the quality".
    "Overall, 6,539 removals were made (22.5% of completed entries"; attention checks used in 47 of 65 data sets;
    one removal reason was "control items, e.g., attention checks (28, 32%)". Lab A completion 97.7% vs Lab B 74.7%.
    Ethics: "Ethics Review Committee for Research Involving Human Subjects, Tokyo Institute of Technology".
    Guidelines: "Check the quality", "Use attention and technical checks", "Check for earnest/careless
    participation", "Use multiple recruitment calls for the same study" (e.g. "200 general, 100 women, 50 men").
    The multimedia link is called "preliminary and exploratory" by the authors.
  - For us: in Japan's largest pool, about 1 in 5 completed entries was thrown out after quality checks; studies
    with sound or video kept more. Our VR rooms are all multimedia, which points the same way (unverified for VR).

### 2-CN. Chinese online platforms (Credamo, WJX and others): replications and data quality

- Wang, Nelson, Gao, Jung & Hung (2022), SJDM poster, "Validating a Tool to Dramatically Increase the Diversity of
  Samples in Psychological Research" (Fudan, UC Berkeley, NYU, CUHK-Shenzhen). Poster read in full (PDF image):
  https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/presentations/2022-Poster-Wang-Liman-validating-data-source.pdf
  - "An online Chinese survey platform (Credamo), boasts more than 3 million registered workers"; "nine studies
    (N = 11,778)"; "Credamo can provide attentive subjects and valid data".
  - Framing (Asian disease) replicated; "Attention check pass rate: Mturk: 83%; Credamo: 89%"; "Cellphone users:
    Mturk 1.5%; Credamo: 95%"; "Nine months later, study 1b replicated the framing effect."
  - A failure: self-other enjoyment estimates, "Credamo did not!" replicate (six items differed, "three of those were
    in the opposite direction"). Translation mattered: one Openness item's reliability on Credamo was r = .27.
  - Poster, not peer-reviewed.
- Wang, Gao, Jung, Hung & Nelson (2023), SJDM poster, "Western researchers can and should be accessing Eastern
  samples: scientific validation and practical guidance". Read in full (PDF image):
  https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/presentations/2023-Poster-Wang-Liman-Eastern-Sample-Validation.pdf
  - "eleven pre-registered experiments (N = 18,534), we demonstrate that Chinese online platforms yield high-quality
    data"; platforms compared include Credamo (N = 939), WJX (N = 808) and others in Study 1.
  - "for Instrumental manipulation checks 89.9% of Chinese and 89.4% of Americans passed; for the stimulus recall
    check, 99.8% of Chinese and 94.1% of Americans passed."
  - Stroop with Chinese participants and Chinese colour words: Cohen's d = 0.95 (US participants, English: 0.80).
  - Test-retest of Big Five: "high test-retest reliability within samples from both countries".
  - For us: almost all Chinese online participants answer on phones (95%) and still pass attention checks at
    US-or-better rates; a home sample in China is workable; translation is the weak point, not attention.
- Chen Guoqiu, Gao Xiaoxue, Yan Xinyuan, Du Meng, Zang Yinyin & Wang Yin (2023), Journal of Psychological Science
  46(5), 1262, "Online Research in Psychology and Its Future in China" (Beijing Normal University and others).
  Abstract page read: https://jps.ecnu.edu.cn/CN/Y2023/V46/I5/1262 — infrastructure "implemented in NaoDao website
  (www.naodao.com), an online empirical research platform"; the review "suggested that the online data quality can be
  as precise and robust as traditional lab experiments"; China started late, with few online communities and tools
  (paraphrase of the abstract). Full text not read (PDF link not exposed).

### 4-KR. Ethics approval in South Korea for a team without a university: the public IRB

- National Institute for Bioethics Policy, "Operation of the public institutional bioethics committee", read:
  https://nibp.kr/page/portal206
  - What it is: under "Article 12(1) of the Bioethics and Safety Act", a committee "designated by the Minister of
    Health and Welfare" for shared use by institutions or researchers (our translation).
  - Who: "researchers not affiliated with an educational or research institution or hospital" (our translation);
    also institutions where their own committee is practically impossible; institutions may sign an agreement if
    they have 5 or fewer researchers or 30 or fewer reviews in 3 years.
  - Fees (new review, KRW): commercial or funded 200,000; non-profit with funding 100,000; no funding 50,000;
    exemption review 50,000; multi-site 400,000; continuing review 20,000; institutional agreement 1,750,000.
  - Timing: regular review five times a month; papers complete one week before; expedited review any time; only the
    principal investigator may apply, via the e-IRB system (address printed both as public.irb.or.kr and a misspelt
    form on the page).
  - For us: Korea is the clearest case found anywhere so far: a state-run IRB that by law takes unaffiliated
    researchers, for about 50,000-200,000 KRW per study. Whether a Korean public IRB would review a study run by a
    foreign team on Korean players: unverified.

### 4-JP. Ethics approval in Japan for a team without a university: a private psychology ethics committee

- IdeaLab Inc. (Shibuya, Tokyo), service page read: https://idealab.co.jp/rinrishinsa/ — "a research ethics review
  committee for psychology surveys and experiments so that people in a wide range of positions, such as independent
  researchers and corporate researchers, can apply" (our translation); "a research ethics review that anyone can
  apply for" (page heading, our translation).
- Committee page read: https://rinri.idealab.co.jp/ — "Ethics Review Committee for Psychological Research and
  Experiments"; covers "questionnaires, interviews, observation surveys, done face to face or online" and
  "psychology experiments done in a laboratory or online" and "research that measures physiological data" (our
  translation); invasive methods excluded; "in principle, research conducted in Japan by the main investigator" (our
  translation), so a foreign team would need a Japanese principal investigator (inference).
  - Fee: "200,000 yen per application (before tax)"; expedited re-review 110,000 yen; "review period: at most 5
    weeks" (our translation); review by circulation in writing; follows law, Helsinki, Nuremberg, Belmont and the
    Japanese Psychological Association code.
  - Not stated: member count, number of reviews done, which journals accept its approvals.
- Japanese Psychological Association ethics code PDF: the linked file returned 404
  (https://psych.or.jp/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/rinri_kitei.pdf%20); clauses on online studies: not read.

### 4-CN. Ethics approval in mainland China for a team without a university

- Measures for Science and Technology Ethics Review (Trial), Ministry of Science and Technology and others, read on
  the State Council site: https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202310/content_6908045.htm
  - Scope, Art. 2(1): "activities involving humans as research participants, including those who are subjects of
    human testing, surveys, or observational studies, and activities using ... personal information data" (our
    translation). A psychology experiment run in a game falls here.
  - Art. 4: units in "life sciences, medicine, or AI" with ethically sensitive research must set up a committee;
    others may (our translation).
  - Art. 13: if "the unit has no committee, or if no unit staff are conducting the activity, the review must be
    entrusted in writing to another qualified committee" (our translation). So an independent team must contract an
    existing committee in writing.
  - Art. 7: committee "at least seven members", with outside members and both genders (our translation).
  - Art. 22: a simple (expedited) procedure where risk is "no higher than minimal risk" (our translation).
  - In force: "December 1, 2023" (Art. 56).
  - For us: China has a written route for unaffiliated researchers (entrust a committee), but no state-run public
    committee like Korea's was found; which committees accept outside psychology studies, and at what fee: not found.

### 4-TW. Ethics approval in Taiwan for a team without a university

- National Taiwan University research ethics FAQ, read: https://ord.ntu.edu.tw/w/ordntu/info_20082319271076440
  - Two tiers: for "human body research" (the Human Subjects Research Act) review is legally required; for "human
    research" in general "the law does not mandate review, but it is recommended before the study begins" (our
    translation). A behavioural game study with no biological samples likely sits in the second tier (inference).
  - Act Art. 5: review "should be conducted by the committee the research institution establishes. If it has none,
    it may entrust another committee" (our translation).
  - NTU: "NTU REC does not accept applications from non-partner units, visiting scholars, or exchange students" (our
    translation).
  - Exemption: minimal-risk studies may be exempted, but "exemption does not mean no submission" (paraphrase).
- National Tsing Hua University human behaviour and social science REC, fee notice read:
  https://rec.site.nthu.edu.tw/p/404-1233-69340.php?Lang=zh-tw — serves its own staff and students, contracted
  institutions, and "those whom this university has agreed to review on a substitute basis" (our translation).
  Fees for outside institutions or projects (NT$): general 19,500; expedited 14,500; exempt 2,500 (last approval
  date shown: 9 April 2014).
- For us: in Taiwan an outside team can buy review from a university REC that agrees (NTHU lists a price);
  NTU refuses non-partners. Whether NTHU would take a foreign company: unverified.

### 4-SG. Singapore: when a VR psychology study counts as regulated biomedical research

- Human Biomedical Research Act 2015, s.3, read in the browser (WebFetch got 403):
  https://sso.agc.gov.sg/Act/HBRA2015?ProvIds=pr3- — covered research includes study of "(c) the performance or
  endurance of human individuals" where it involves "subjecting an individual to any intervention (including any
  wilful act or omission) that has a physical, mental or physiological effect"; such research must be reviewed by
  "an institutional review board appointed by that research institution" (s.3(5)). The Second Schedule exemptions
  were not read.
- For us: a measured VR task of performance could fall under the Act's wording (our reading, not legal advice;
  unverified against the Second Schedule and Ministry of Health guidance). Social-behavioural studies at
  universities are reviewed by their IRBs under separate guidelines (SUTD IRB page in a search listing only:
  https://sutd.edu.sg/research/institutional-review-board, unverified).
- 4-HK. Hong Kong: no statute or public route for unaffiliated researchers found (not searched in depth).

### 2-VR. VR outside the lab (found while searching for Asia; these two are NOT Asian, and new to our files)

- Gottsacker, Bruder, ... Bailenson (2026), arXiv 2609.26203, "Beyond the Lab: Large-Scale Remote Cybersickness
  Research in Virtual Reality Using the VERA Platform" (University of Central Florida and others). Read:
  https://arxiv.org/html/2609.26203v1
  - Prolific, owners of Quest 2/3/3S, 18-65; paid $15/hour pro-rated (about $7.50 for 30 min); browser link, no
    researcher present; consent form told them the study aimed to induce cybersickness.
  - Funnel: 912 pre-questionnaire, 381 launched VR (41.8%), 288 completed (75.6% of launchers), 10 failed quality
    checks, final 263 (28.8% of 912). "roughly 3,500 questionnaire completers" needed for 1,000 analysable.
  - "Across cohorts, cybersickness outcomes were consistent in direction and temporal pattern" with the in-lab
    study (N = 30); remote found age and sex effects the lab sample could not. "remote VR studies can serve as a
    valid complement to controlled laboratory research."
- Quiros-Ramirez, Feineisen, Streuber & Reips (2025), PLOS ONE 10.1371/journal.pone.0318688, "Virtual Reality
  experiments in the field" (Konstanz, Germany). Read:
  https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0318688 — 60 participants, Cardboard posted
  home vs lab; "No significant difference in participants' sense of presence, cybersickness, perceived stress and
  relaxation were detected"; "close-to-zero dropout rate throughout conditions".

### 2-VR-JP. Japan: perception experiments run inside social VR (VRChat, cluster) on players' own setups

- Kawaguchi, Tamegai, Hayashi & Satou (2026), 31st VRSJ Annual Conference, paper 2C1-12, "Effects of other
  avatars' appearance and body ownership on phantom sense in social VR" (Kansai University, Tokyo Denki University;
  technical report, not peer-reviewed). Read in full (3 pages, PDF image):
  https://www.conference.vrsj.org/ac2026/program/doc/2C1-12.pdf
  - Run entirely in VRChat: "The experiment was carried out on VRChat" (our translation); participants
    "accessed the experiment from the environment where they normally use VR" (our translation), i.e. own headsets.
  - Recruiting: open calls "in VRChat communities on X (formerly Twitter), Misskey and Discord, and also inside
    VRChat" (our translation); 23 participants who self-identify as having phantom sense (up from 12 in the
    earlier study, which the authors credit for the now-significant mirror effect).
  - Control at home: participants were asked to switch off overlay software and VRChat's face and personal
    mirrors, and to set mirror resolution to "Full" (our translation). The world used a gimmick "VERS" for running
    conditions and collecting questionnaires (Tamegai & Satou 2026, "VERS: a support tool unifying experiment
    definition and questionnaire collection in VRChat", FIT 2026 K-017; not read).
  - Reward, consent and ethics: not stated in the paper (not found).
  - Results: mirror on/off F(1,198) = 17.15, p < .001; other-avatar appearance F(4,198) = 4.61, p = .001.
  - Background cited: a community survey of about 2,000 social-VR users found 26% "often" and 17% "sometimes" feel
    phantom touch (Nemchan & Bredikhina 2023, a note.com post, not read).
- Hu Yong Hao, Hatada, Narumi (University of Tokyo) & Hiraki (Cluster Metaverse Research Institute) (2024), VRSJ
  2A1-11, "Researcher's Bar: an experiment platform and implementation templates using the metaverse". Listing
  read: https://www.conference.vrsj.org/ac2024/program/program-flash02.html — "an experiment platform in the
  metaverse and implementation templates for the experiments run on it" to speed up "participant recruitment,
  experiment creation, and experiment execution"; prototype on the cluster service (our translation). Full paper
  not read; numbers of participants: not found.
- For us: Japan has a working pattern we had not seen: recruit inside the VR community itself (VRChat groups on
  X, Discord, Misskey) and run the room in the players' own world; a platform company (cluster) runs a research
  institute that builds experiment templates.
- Koyanagi, Narumi & Ohmura (2020), Transactions of the VR Society of Japan 25(1), 50-59, "Improving body ownership
  and quality of experience in social VR with the avatar one uses every day" (Toyohashi University of Technology,
  University of Tokyo). Full text read (PDF): https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/tvrsj/25/1/25_50/_pdf/-char/ja
  (saved: objekt-papers/koyanagi-2020.txt)
  - Experiments 1 and 2 run online inside VRChat on users' own equipment: "36 VRChat users took part after giving
    consent" (Exp. 1); "21 VRChat users took part after giving consent" (Exp. 2) (our translation).
  - Screening: 4 of 36 (and 3 of 21) excluded for under 30 hours of play, the paper's definition of a "self-avatar"
    user. Call text asked them to "come to the experiment using the avatar you usually use" (our translation).
  - Home control: movement by controller only, "so as not to depend on the participant's home environment"; "since
    the experiment is online, full control of the environment across participants is difficult" (our translation).
  - Measures: a 2-minute cube-picking task, then a questionnaire on a survey site after each trial. They did NOT
    use task performance, partly because avatars change behaviour and "there is a concern that eagerness for the
    experiment drops" with the avatars users mostly wear (our translation).
  - Why Exp. 3 moved to the lab: online questionnaire answers might be a subjective rating interpreted "meta"-ly,
    "it is my avatar, so it is my body" (our translation); so they brought VRChat users to the lab for skin
    conductance with written consent. Reward and ethics committee for the online parts: not stated (not found).
  - For us: a Japanese team found that home VR answers can reflect what players think they should feel, and
    added a physiological check in the lab. Our room can add a behavioural measure (head, hands) for the same
    reason.
