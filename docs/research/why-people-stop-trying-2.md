# Why people stop trying (part 2): short opening messages, rooms, and the game

Continues why-people-stop-trying.md (same rules: read: full / abstract only / unverified). Run 12.10.2026.

## Part 2 findings

### 2.1 Awe — it is easy to induce (also in VR); what it does afterwards is weakly shown

- Piff, Dietze, Feinberg, Stancato & Keltner 2015, "Awe, the small self, and prosocial behavior", JPSP
  108(6):883-899, doi:10.1037/pspi0000018. Read: abstract only (Consensus record; no open full text
  found; APA press copy https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-pspi0000018.pdf returned a
  page, not the PDF). Abstract: 5 studies, "N = 2,078"; Study 5, standing "in a grove of towering
  trees" increased helping and decreased entitlement; small self explains the effect "in part". No
  numbers taken. Paywall: for the owner.
- Replication status: no direct preregistered replication of Piff 2015 found in this run. Luo, Yan &
  Yuan 2023 (Stress and Brain, read: abstract only) say the small-self mediation "has not been
  consistently replicated". Two 2024 meta-analyses of awe and prosocial behaviour (Li et al., Curr
  Psychol, 57 effect sizes, N = 20,997; Gao et al., Basic Appl Soc Psych, r = 0.40) were seen only as
  abstracts; both pool correlational with experimental data, so they do not show that inducing awe
  changes behaviour. Unverified.
- In VR, awe itself is reliably raised by vast scenes: Chirico et al. 2018 (library card
  `docs/cards/chirico-2018.md`, read: full) — awe scenes above a neutral meadow; mountains highest;
  Earth from deep space median 5 of 7 on a single item, N = 36.
- Overview effect: Yaden et al. 2016 (Psychology of Consciousness 3:1-11; read: abstract only) is an
  analysis of astronaut accounts, not an experiment. van Horen et al. 2024, PLOS ONE,
  doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0299883. Read: full (library `van-horen-2024.txt`): N = 145 (Study 1);
  "Results show no effects of the short (7 minutes) overview effect VR video on pro-environmental
  behaviour"; with 15 min (Study 2) the most immersive version raised donations "but not
  significantly". Loy et al. 2024 (J Environ Psychol, preregistered, N = 128, read: abstract only): VR
  space tour raised awe but had no direct effect on global identity or petition signing. Krug et al.
  2026 scoping review (read: abstract only): simulation studies "have yielded inconclusive results".
- Status: a projector scene of the Earth from space can be expected to move people (awe ratings rise);
  it should not be expected, or claimed, to change what they do next.

### 2.3 Mortality and rarity framing — the classic mortality-salience effect failed a large replication

- Klein et al. 2022, "Many Labs 4: Failure to replicate mortality salience effect with and without
  original author involvement", Collabra: Psychology 8(1):35271, doi:10.1525/collabra.35271. Read: full
  (PsyArXiv preprint text, OSF file of doi:10.31234/osf.io/vef2c, library `klein-2022-manylabs4.txt`;
  the journal page is behind a bot check). Replicated Greenberg et al. 1994 Study 1 (write about your own
  death vs a control topic, then rate pro- and anti-American essay authors). "21 labs", 2,281 people
  collected, 17 labs and 1,578 after preregistered exclusions. Result: "Hedges' g = 0.07, 95% CI =
  [-0.03, 0.17]" (set 1), no heterogeneity across labs; "original d = 1.34; replication d ranged from
  0.07 to 0.13". The authors do not call the original necessarily false: history or a much smaller true
  effect are their alternatives.
- What the owner's opening is closer to is not mortality salience but the rarity of being alive (a
  "lottery ticket"). No experiment testing a "your existence is improbable" message on later effort or
  ambition was found in this run: not found. Unverified as an effect.
- Status: a reminder of death changing what people then do is not a safe claim; the best test found a
  near-zero effect. A rarity message can be shown as a fact about the world (sourced), not as something
  that will change the player.

### 2.4 Self-affirmation — small, fragile at scale

- Hanselman, Rozek, Grigg & Borman 2017, J Educ Psychol 109(3):405-424, doi:10.1037/edu0000141,
  PMC5403146. Read: full (library `hanselman-2017.txt`). A well-powered replication "in a setting where a
  previous large-scale field experiment demonstrated significant positive impacts, using the same
  procedures": "We found no evidence of effects", precise enough "to reject benefits larger than an effect
  size of 0.10"; "the potential fragility of self-affirmation in educational settings when implemented
  widely".
- Wu, Spreckelsen & Cohen 2021 meta-analysis (58 studies; Hedges' g = .15 for identity-threatened
  students, .01 for others) — read: search snippet only, unverified.
- Status: an affirmation exercise (writing about one's values) helps at most a little, mostly for people
  under a specific threat, and failed when scaled. Not a basis for a promise to players.

### 2.5 Wise interventions (Walton) — precise, short, and they work only where the setting lets them

- Walton et al. 2023, "Where and with whom does a brief social-belonging intervention promote progress in
  college?", Science 380:499-505, doi:10.1126/science.ade4420, erratum 12 Sep 2024 (table S6 only, "not any
  other tables or figures ... nor any conclusions"). Read: full, 7 pages and the erratum, from the owner's
  phone screenshots of 12.10 (objekt-files/notes/why-people-2026-10-12/walton-2023-owner-screenshots/).
  Double-blind randomized trial, "26,911 students" at 22 institutions (p. 1); a "brief (10 to 30 min)
  reading-and-writing activity" (p. 1); the key writing task took "just 7 min 36 s (median)" (p. 3). Outcome:
  completing the first year enrolled full-time. Effects varied across local-identity groups, SD "2.4 percentage
  points" (p. 4). Where the group was afforded belonging (85% of students), the average effect was "1.1
  percentage points" (p. 4), largest for groups with low historic achievement, "2.0 percentage points" (p. 5),
  essentially zero for high-achieving groups; where belonging was not afforded, no effect (pp. 4-5, Fig. 3).
  The authors: the potential "depends on the opportunity students have to belong in their college contexts" (p. 6).
- Walton & Wilson 2018, "Wise interventions", Psychol Rev — not read (not found open in this run).
- How these messages are built, read in full in Yeager 2019 (above): the idea is not told as an order —
  "students were not told outright that they should work hard"; people "internalize the message by
  teaching it to a future first-year ninth grade student"; stories from older students. Effects were
  0.1 SD and depended on the surroundings.
- Status: the evidence for short framing that changes later behaviour comes from exercises of 10-60 min aimed at
  one specific worry: Walton 2023, 10-30 min, raised first-year full-time completion by 1.1 percentage points
  where the college afforded belonging and not at all where it did not; Yeager 2019, about 0.1 SD. A single
  opening slide is not that.

### 2.6 Backfire: reactance and demand

- Reactance (Brehm 1966, not read). Two 2025 meta-analyses, read: abstract only (Consensus records):
  Li et al. 2025, Hum Commun Res (33 studies): "high freedom-threatening language increased ... psychological
  reactance (k = 53, r = .20)"; anger and negative thoughts were "negatively associated with persuasion
  outcomes". Chu et al. 2026, J Advertising (142 samples): assertive language raised freedom threat
  (d = 0.72) and reactance (d = 0.39) and slightly lowered persuasion (d = -0.08). Unverified beyond
  abstracts; the direction is consistent: telling people what they should do costs a little persuasion.
- Demand: Orne 1962, Am Psychol 17:776-783 (library `orne-1962.txt`, read: full earlier for the library):
  a participant "sees it as his task to ascertain the true purpose of the experiment and respond in a
  manner which will support the hypotheses being tested". An opening that announces "you can do more than
  you think" tells the player the hypothesis of every room after it, which can change the rooms' own
  results (players try harder because they were told to, not because of the room's manipulation).

### 2.6a Backfire of encouraging statements — "positive self-statements" (abstract-level only)

- Wood, Perunovic & Lee 2009, "Positive self-statements", Psychol Sci 20:860-866,
  doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02370.x (Crossref). Read: abstract only (Consensus record): among low self-esteem people,
  repeating "I'm a lovable person" made them "feel worse"; "backfire for the very people who 'need' them
  the most".
- Flynn & Bordieri 2020, J Contextual Behav Sci, doi:10.1016/j.jcbs.2020.03.003 (Crossref). Read: abstract only: two
  replications, about 225 and 237 people: "a failure to replicate as there were no difference between
  conditions" and self-esteem "did not interact".
- Zhu et al. 2021, Br J Health Psychol, N = 422. Read: abstract only: self-affirmation helped high
  self-esteem people accept a health message and had "adverse effects" for low self-esteem people.
- Status: unsettled; the backfire is not reliably shown, nor is benefit. The safe reading for the game:
  a flattering line addressed to the player ("you can do much more") has no tested benefit and a
  possible cost for the players who feel least able. Unverified beyond abstracts.

## Rooms the research points to (faithful re-creation: clear task, about 10 min, published procedure)

| Wilson 2014 thinking period (1.8) | Sit alone 15 min; a button gives an unpleasant stimulus | 6-15 min | Enjoyment part replicated in 11 countries; shock rates replicated (63-89%), the reason did not | No shock in VR; a long empty wait in a headset |

First candidate on this evidence: the Deci free-choice room. It is the only one whose effect held across
about a hundred studies, whose measure is hidden and automatic (seconds of free play while "alone"), and
whose reveal speaks to the owner's question without flattering: "you kept playing — or stopped — when the
reason moved outside you". A new-room card (new-room skill) and a reading of a one-session protocol are
needed before any build; the praise arm (positive feedback raises free play) avoids paying money.

## What this means for the game

The projector opening (board item of 12.10, the lottery ticket):
- What can be said as fact, with a source in docs/sources.md: that Earth is the only planet where life is
  known (needs an astronomy source, not found in this run: unverified here); that people prefer doing
  something to doing nothing (Wilson 2014; 11-country replication); that effort feels costly to almost
  everyone (David 2024, abstract only); that passivity in the face of what one cannot control is the body's
  default and control is learned (Maier & Seligman 2016, animal evidence, said as such).
- What it should not claim: that the message will change the player. No study read here shows a one-screen
  opening changing later behaviour: mortality salience g = 0.07 (Many Labs 4); overview-effect VR no
  significant behaviour change (van Horen 2024); growth-mindset teaching about 0.1 SD after two 25-min
  sessions, for struggling students only (Yeager 2019; Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023: nonsignificant after bias
  correction). Nor that "everyone has hidden abilities": no study measured that.
- Form: an awe image (Earth from space) reliably raises awe in VR (Chirico 2018); say the fact, do not
  instruct. Telling people what they should do costs a little persuasion (reactance, abstracts only), and
  announcing "you can do more than you think" hands the player the hypothesis of every room after it
  (Orne 1962, demand), which can bend the rooms' own data.
- A flattering line has no tested benefit and may hurt those who feel least able (Wood 2009; failed
  replication Flynn 2020; abstracts only): leave it out.

What the rooms can honestly show:
- A player's own measured moment (how long he kept going, how much control he felt), next to what the
  paper found, with the replication record beside it (held / did not hold). Confidence follows a seen
  success more than it causes one (Sitzmann & Yeo 2013, abstract only): the rooms' reveal, not the
  projector, is where "you can do more" could be earned.

What to avoid:
- "Sadder but wiser", ego depletion, "awe makes you generous", "a reminder of death changes you", "growth
  mindset transforms results", "you would rather hurt yourself than think" — each failed or is disputed above.
- Loud aversive sound (Hiroto's 110 dB), depression screening, real deception about failure without a
  debrief.
