## Findings

### From papers already on disk
- A4 LabintheWild (objekt-papers/reinecke-2015.txt, CSCW 2015). Launch July 2012 through the authors' own
  networks: "we first advertised three studies through our personal social networks on Facebook, Twitter, and
  Google+". Then press came by itself: "we observed an increase in mentions of LabintheWild in social shares, on
  blog posts, and in online newspaper articles". Facebook page added April 2013, email sign-up July 2013. No paid
  ads: "We do not otherwise actively advertise or recruit participants." Sharing buttons "including the first and
  last page of each experiment". Cross-promotion: "Below their personalized results, we suggest two other
  LabintheWild experiments" so "our most popular experiments end up generating traffic for those that are less
  popular". Facebook = top referrer; "more than 5,000 websites" link to it; referrers include huffingtonpost.co.uk,
  dailymail.co.uk, reddit.com, Hungarian and Lithuanian news sites (Table 2). The measure of sharing is
  qualitative (200 tweets coded), not an experiment: two motives to share, altruism ("#science is cool") and
  showing off a discovery ("one of them will guess your age by how you click on the red dot"). Key claim: "The
  feedback provided by the experiments also appears to be the feature that most influences people's decision to
  share". A one-line ask worked: telling people to invite "especially people over 50" and "the average age of
  participants noticeably increased". Limits: "It typically takes a few weeks for a new study to receive as much
  traffic as the established ones"; length kept 5-15 min. Scale: "more than 1,000 participants on an average day".
- A6 Great Brain Experiment (objekt-papers/brown-2014.txt, PLoS ONE 2014). Launch "middle of March 2013";
  "Publicity was garnered through blog posts and a number of print articles. The social media sharing function
  within the app generated word-of-mouth publicity." Rate: "a sixteen-fold increase in the rate of data
  collection over a previous attempt at smartphone data collection"; they credit "'gamification'" and "efforts to
  package the app in a stylish, engaging format"; "extensively discussed on Twitter and reviewed favourably on
  blogs such as the Wall Street Journal Speakeasy blog" (WSJ "top 10 android games", 17 Apr 2013, ref 19).
  Cost of keeping reach: "recruitment and retention of participants required commitment to maintain the high
  profile of the app through both traditional and social media." Shortest game = most plays: "the quickest game
  (the stop-signal reaction time) yielded, and continues to yield, the highest number of plays".
- C1 Great Brain Experiment game wrapping (brown-2014.txt): "the strategy which produced optimal experimental data
  should be congruent with the category giving a high score"; warns illusions break this ("participants could
  maximise their points by feigning veridical perception"). Easy first level kept despite no data: "starting with
  a very easy level reduces the need for complicated explanations ... and encourages participants to persevere";
  "Forced delays were kept to a minimum".
- C2 Lumsden et al. 2016 (objekt-papers/lumsden-2016.txt): points = same data, more enjoyment ("points to be a
  highly suitable game mechanic ... they do not disrupt the validity of the data collected and yet increase
  participant enjoyment"); the cowboy narrative theme changed the data ("The clear differences between the theme
  and non-game variants invalidates the use of these stimuli for gathering data comparable to non-gamelike GNG
  tasks"); "the addition of even a single gamelike feature makes a huge difference to the participants'
  perception of the task".

### A2 Moral Machine
- "Inside the Moral Machine" by the authors, Springer Nature Behind the Paper blog
  (https://communities.springernature.com/posts/inside-the-moral-machine), via WebFetch:
  - Not built as a mass product: "On June 23rd, 2016, we deployed Moral Machine." "The website was intended to be
    a mere companion survey to a paper being published that day" (Bonnefon, Shariff, Rahwan, Science 2016).
  - Press drove the first surge: "traffic poured in beyond the capacity of the website"; "Thirty minutes later, it
    crashed."
  - Biggest spike: "The biggest one was in early October of that year", from a "front page of Reddit - twice".
  - Repeated waves: "It subsequently went viral on multiple occasions", via international news, "mega-popular
    livestream celebrities in the playthrough/reaction video genre", and "national publications with niche
    readership bases around the world" (no streamer named on this page).
  - Built to spread: "engineered to encourage sharing, and modeled on personality quizzes"; "(yes, we generated
    language-specific sharing widgets too)"; translated into nine more languages "by year-end"; "very popular on
    Japanese Twitter and on Russian-language VK".
  - Scale: "40 million human decisions ... within the span of 18 months", from "233" countries and territories.
- PRIMARY, the scientists' own account: Spiers, Coutrot & Hornberger (2023), Topics in Cognitive Science 15(1),
  doi:10.1111/tops.12590; accepted manuscript read in full and saved as objekt-papers/spiers-2023.txt
  (https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10140403/3/Spiers_CoutrotHornberger2021Manuscript_revised.pdf).
  - Origin: "The project arose from the intention of Deutsche Telekom (T-mobile) to engage people with a" good
    cause; Saatchi & Saatchi and Glitchers built "the navigation test embedded in the video game".
  - How the millions came, in their words: Saatchi made "several film and animation adverts about the study with
    high end production values and a developing emotive story linked to dementia to motivate players to join";
    in-game sharing: "share progress and the upgrading of their boat via Facebook and Twitter"; "T-mobile
    specifically advertised the game to its millions of customers. Alzheimer's research UK promoted it to its
    supporters"; "Pewdiepie, was recruited to publish a promotional video, resulting in over 2 million views in 5
    days"; result: "the most downloaded app on the Apple App store for a day and creating a 'moment' on Twitter.
    This snowball effect is what we attributed to being able to recruit 3.9 million participants".
- C1 Sea Hero Quest wrapping (same paper):
  - Boat chosen by the studio (Glitchers) for two reasons: steering a boat is hard in real life so clumsy controls
    feel natural, and "with water it is clear where you can go and where you cannot go".
  - Morris Water Maze rejected: circling "could potentially cause nausea, and the repetitive experience would be
    less engaging". Kept three standard tasks: wayfinding (map shown, then hidden), path integration ("flare
    levels": shoot a flare back to the start), spatial working memory (radial maze as "bonus levels").
  - Reward tied to the measure: "rewarded three stars if they reached the checkpoints quickly, two if slower";
    too slow -> an arrow appears and one star.
  - Pure-fun levels, not recorded: every 5th level a sea-monster chase and photo "to vary the game play and create
    photographs that could allow users to share progress"; "The data from these levels was not recorded".
  - Game skill separated from the measure: tutorial levels 1-2 have "no choices and no need for spatial
    navigation"; trajectories normalized by them. Validity: game vs real streets "in London r = 0.46 ... in Paris
    r = 0.57"; tutorial vs real world "r = 0.06".
  - Cost of fun play: filter "less dedicated players, for instance the ones who played a couple of levels with
    their friends around a beer"; kept those who finished the first 6 wayfinding + 2 flare levels and gave
    demographics ("57% of the total"); age spikes "at 18 and 99" discarded (fake ages).

### A3 Project Implicit
- Golden Goose Award profile (https://www.goldengooseaward.org/01awardees/implicit-bias), via WebFetch: the site
  was made so "more people could try out the tests" - "They are so interesting to do"; Nosek "barely slept for six
  weeks"; "When the website went live in 1998, the team began seeing thousands of users a day"; "Within the first
  month, they had received 50,000 completed tests." Why people came: "People cared about the tests results";
  "The simplicity of the implicit bias test ... was the key to its success." Book "Blindspot" (2013) took the idea
  to the public. Funding: NSF, later NIH. No press release or launch campaign is named on this page.
- Later totals: Harvard Gazette, 26 Feb 2013 (https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2013/02/peering-into-our-blind-spots/):
  "In the past 15 years, more than 14 million such tests have been taken"; Banaji "hoped to garner 500 responses in
  the first year", and "With no advertising, they hit 45,000 in the first month". Ratliff & Smith, Daedalus Winter
  2024, read in full, saved objekt-papers/ratliff-2024.txt (https://www.amacad.org/publication/daedalus/implicit-association-test): "As of
  late 2023, more than eighty million study sessions have been launched and more than forty million IATs
  completed", "an IAT every twenty-one seconds"; the site is "set up in the model of an interactive exhibit at a
  science museum". ABC News 2009 (11 million) and the Cambridge Handbook 2024 (28 million): not opened, superseded.

### A5 TestMyBrain
- Harvard Gazette, 31 Aug 2012 (https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2012/08/bringing-the-psych-lab-online),
  via WebFetch: "recruited thousands of volunteers through Germine's site, TestMyBrain.org"; they "navigated from
  search engines and social networking sites"; came "to learn more about themselves and contribute to scientific
  research"; Germine: "the results from studies conducted with Web volunteers were the same as those done in the
  lab"; "it's fast, it's cheap, but it's not dirty". Paper: Germine et al. 2012, Psychon Bull Rev 19(5):847-857,
  read in full (objekt-papers/germine-2012.txt): "collected data from half a million participants over the past
  three years"; top traffic sources "www.google.com, www.stumbleupon.com, and www.i-am-bored.com"; "brain test"
  searches = 13% of traffic; reward = "feedback on how their performance compared with the performance of the
  average participant". No dated jumps found.

### A7 Foldit
- Cooper et al. (2010) "The challenge of designing scientific discovery games", FDG 2010, read in full and saved
  as objekt-papers/cooper-2010.txt (https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/scooper/index_files/pub/cooper2010challenge.pdf).
  Public release "May 2008"; authors include ">57,000 Foldit players". Fun is a means: "the requirement that the
  game be fun, contribute to achieving this goal, as the results rely on players playing the game".
  - Score = the science: "we base scoring on the Rosetta energy function ... we negate the energy so players are
    competing for a higher score"; "we organize the game in the form of a competition"; separate soloist and
    evolver leaderboards after a fairness problem. Social: "in-game chat, a website with forums, and a
    player-created wiki". Look: "a bright, cartoonish look"; "not bring back memories of high school textbooks".
  - Teaching: introductory levels where "using the newly introduced concepts is the easiest way to reach the goal
    score"; tuned by think-alouds and per-level completion data. The paper says nothing on press or reach.
- The 2011 result: Khatib et al., "Crystal structure of a monomeric retroviral protease solved by protein folding
  game players", Nat Struct Mol Biol 18(10):1175-1177, 18 Sep 2011 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3705907/,
  via WebFetch): solved in a "3-week competition"; no player count. Press pages on it (National Geographic, JHU):
  not opened. Total: "Since its release, Foldit has gained over 460,000 players" (Koepnick, UW Center for Game
  Science, NSF STEM for All video hall 2017, https://stemforall2017.videohall.com/presentations/1032.html). Active
  players: the page gives none; the forum estimate (under 3,000) is a player's post, not a source. No dated curve.

### B2 Small reach for comparison: Science XL lexical decision app (Dufau et al. 2011, PLoS ONE 6(9):e24974)
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3182196, via WebFetch: launched "December 2010"; "4157 emails received
  during the four-month period following launch"; "organized as a word game" but the only promotion named is the
  store text ("Information about the nature of the word game was given in the iTunes App Store"); feedback = "mean
  accuracy and response time of the session", no comparison with others named. Brown et al. 2014 call the Great
  Brain Experiment "a sixteen-fold increase in the rate of data collection" over this attempt (their ref. 18 is
  Dufau 2011). Same kind of task, same platform: the difference was game packaging, sharing, a comparison with
  other players, press and Twitter attention (Brown's own explanation).
- EyeWire's own early phase (35-50 players a day before deliberate outreach, above) is the second documented case;
  LabintheWild notes new studies need "a few weeks" to catch up even inside a known site.

### C2 Narrative, humour, points and data (beyond Lumsden 2016)
- Friehs, Dechant, Vedress, Frings & Mandryk (2020), JMIR Serious Games 8(3):e17810, doi:10.2196/17810; full
  text read and saved as objekt-papers/friehs-2020.txt (Europe PMC PMC7509611).
  - A story-only gamification (no points): "we did not use points, scores, or a win or loss condition ... but
    employed narrative elements including a backstory, a theme, and characters along with immersive elements of a
    3D world". Premise built into the instructions: "Once upon a time you have been lured to an enchanted forest by
    an evil witch"; the arrow became a pointing fairy; the stop beep = the witch in disguise. Same code with a
    toggle "to keep the implementation of the underlying task identical"; stimulus kept "easily discriminable".
  - Data unchanged: "the behavioral performance was comparable between the tasks (P<.87; BF01=2.87)"; study 2
    SSRT "F1,28=0.03; P=.87" (lab, about 30 people). Enjoyment higher in study 1, not significant in study 2.
  - Their review of earlier work: points can speed responses and also "increase error rates in a dot-probe task";
    fruit stimuli plus a narrative "resulted in greater stop-signal reaction times (SSRTs; ie, worsened
    performance)"; "a poorly implemented theme that offers little gameplay might be worse for engagement than
    including no theme at all". Their fix: a known genre (infinite runner), "professional quality graphics", the
    story mapped one-to-one onto the task rules.
- Humour, the only randomized test found: Truth Initiative summary of Rath et al. (2016), Social Science Computer
  Review (https://truthinitiative.org/research-resources/tobacco-prevention-efforts/how-humor-can-boost-response-
  rates-online-survey), via WebFetch: "2,963 participants" aged 18-34 who ignored a first invitation; a humorous
  reminder "increased the odds of response to the survey by 23 percent". It measured response, not data quality.
  Humour INSIDE a measured task vs data quality: no peer-reviewed study found (3 searches). Practitioner claim,
  Kantar, 23 May 2023 (https://www.kantar.com/inspiration/research-services/humour-the-deadly-serious-role-it-can-play-in-market-research-pf,
  via WebFetch): after a cartoon, people "admitting to not voting increased from 20% to 40%", "almost exactly in
  line with real life vote turnout levels in the UK"; a toothpaste survey "reduced the amount of straight-lining
  from 50% to nearly zero". No sample size, no control described: a company claim. No Quirk's page was found.
- Narrator in gamified assessment and data: no study found that tests a narrator or host voice against no voice
  (3 searches). Nearest: a protocol for an animated "virtual experimenter" (C4). Closest evidence: Portal
  commentary (voice changes what players do, C3 in science-reach-3.md); Lumsden 2016 and Friehs 2020 (theme matters
  only through what the player sees and does during the trial).

### C4 "The experimenter as a character"
- Hoering, Newsome, Enck, Babu & Muth (2016), "A virtual experimenter to increase standardization for the
  investigation of placebo effects", BMC Med Res Methodol 16:84, read in full, saved objekt-papers/horing-2016.txt:
  a 3D agent "Dr. Halsey" (voice actor, lip-sync, FAQ by touch screen) planned against a human experimenter and
  audio/text. Only pilots ran: N = 48 judged it well on Message and Likeability but it "received comparatively bad
  rating on the Compelling scale", which they put down to "a rather neutral demeanor". No comparison data yet.
- Otherwise not found as a studied topic (2 searches): a fan page on The Stanley Parable narrator, a student blog.
  Portal's commentary (C3, science-reach-3.md) is the best design source: watchers hidden behind frosted glass, the
  subject in a jumpsuit, the AI voice as the only experimenter.

### B1 What the million-scale ones share (from the pages above)
1. An outside megaphone at the start, not "it is science": a brand's paid campaign (Sea Hero Quest: Telekom's
   customers, Saatchi films, PewDiePie, Alzheimer's Research UK's supporters -> 500,000 downloads in 5 days, 3.9
   million in total); press on the day a paper came out, then Reddit's front page twice (Moral Machine); blogs,
   print and WSJ (Great Brain Experiment); the founders' own networks, then press that came by itself (LITW).
2. A result about yourself, compared with others: LITW ("the main factor in motivating participants to recruit
   others"), Moral Machine ("modeled on personality quizzes"), Project Implicit ("People cared about the tests
   results"), TestMyBrain ("to learn more about themselves"), Great Brain Experiment (scores vs other users).
3. Sharing built into the result screen, in the player's language: Moral Machine's language-specific widgets
   (popular on VK and Japanese Twitter); Sea Hero Quest's shareable boat upgrades and monster photos; LITW buttons on
   the first and last page.
4. Free, no sign-up, short: LITW 5-15 min; Great Brain Experiment games under 5 min (the shortest got the most
   plays); Sea Hero Quest 2-5 min a day.
5. Many languages: Moral Machine ten (Russian included); Sea Hero Quest 40 markets.
6. Waves, not one launch: Moral Machine "went viral on multiple occasions"; LITW new studies need "a few weeks";
   Great Brain Experiment had to "maintain the high profile of the app".
- Small reach (B2): the Science XL word app had the same kind of task and platform but none of 2, 3 or 6, and no
  press: 4,157 people in four months vs the Great Brain Experiment's 20,800 in one month. EyeWire had 35-50
  players a day before its press launch (Boston.com), over 200,000 later (case study, A8).

## D. What to copy, ranked by how much it brought those projects
1. A launch megaphone with a news hook: line up a press story and video creators for the day the first floor
   opens (Sea Hero Quest's PewDiePie video, 2+ million views in 5 days, made it the top App Store download for a
   day; Moral Machine's press and Reddit). For Russian speakers the documented channel is VK (Moral Machine). Who
   paid for Sea Hero Quest: Deutsche Telekom (development cost not public; German media budget EUR 82k, A1).
2. Your result vs everyone else at the end of every room, as a one-line, slightly surprising headline you want to
   show (LITW's "Only 3% of people ... share your visual preferences!"). This is the reward and the recruiter.
3. A share button on that result screen, in Russian, plus a one-line ask ("send it to a friend who thinks they
   would not fall for it"); LITW's one-line ask changed who came. The shared picture should not spoil the trick
   (LITW shows "only ... a summary" so answers do not spread).
4. Free, no account, one room = a few minutes; the shortest task gets the most plays.
5. Next-room suggestion under every result (LITW: popular studies feed the less popular ones).
6. Inside the room: score only where a high score = good data (Great Brain Experiment, Foldit, Sea Hero Quest
   stars); fun bits that are not measured are kept out of the record (Sea Hero Quest monster levels); measure skill
   with the controls separately (Sea Hero Quest tutorial levels).
7. Story only if it maps one-to-one onto the task and does not change what is seen during trials (Friehs 2020 ok;
   Lumsden 2016's cowboys changed the data).
8. The host voice: silent during the measured moment, speaks between steps, explains only the hardest step,
   personality grows as the player goes on (Portal commentary). Design evidence only; no study measured a host
   voice's effect on data. Humour has one randomized result, on response rates (reminders, +23% odds).
