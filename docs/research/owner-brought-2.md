## Part 1. Ward & Meade (2023), Annu. Rev. Psychol. 74:577-96, careless responding (CC BY)

Takeaway: careless answers are in nearly every unsupervised survey; preventing them beats deleting them, and what
prevents them best is the belief that carelessness is watched and has consequences, not rewards.

Numbers that matter:
- Prevalence "can range from 1% to 50%"; a review of 48 crowdsourced alcohol studies found a mean of 11.7% (p.582).
- Carelessness rises toward the end of surveys (p.579); people drift in and out of attention rather than being
  careless throughout (p.582).
- Time cut-off: 2 s per item, timed per page, not per survey (Bowling et al. 2016) (pp.585, 588).
- Longstring: Huang et al. (2012) suggest 6 to 14 identical answers in a row; above about 30 is likely careless,
  but 4 vs 6 says nothing; score flags as yes/no, not as a scale (p.588).
- Meade & Craig (2012): item pairs need r >= 0.6 (p.587); in their simulation a mixture model over several flags
  worked best; longstring catches invariant answering, Mahalanobis distance and even-odd consistency catch random
  answering (p.589). Yentes (2020): longstring cut 0.4 SD above the mean; Mahalanobis 0.5 SD (loses many diligent
  people) (p.589). Strict screening can shrink a sample "by 50% or more" (p.589).
- Worst case: over 100 repetitive items, online, dull, no consequences, mandatory (p.591).
Prevention evidence (p.590): Ward & Meade (2018) had people initial statements that they understood careful
answering: consistency, accuracy and interest rose. Warnings and making carelessness feel hypocritical reduce it
(Gibson & Bowling 2020, Huang et al. 2012, Meade & Craig 2012). "Compared to warnings, ... offering rewards ... has
been less effective" (p.590). Virtual and in-person proctoring cut bogus-item failures but not the composite
(Francavilla et al. 2019); an on-screen virtual human reduced carelessness (Ward & Pond 2015); interactive warnings
had no effect (Gibson 2019). Bogus items must not be funny: students agree to be funny (Meade & Craig 2012, p.592);
diligent people sometimes agree with impossible items (Curran & Hauser 2019, p.585).
Not in the text: Berinsky 2016 is not cited anywhere (checked the reference list); no effect sizes for Huang 2012.
Take for our game:
1. Measure, do not ask: per-screen time on every rating screen, an invariance check, one instructed-response item;
   set exclusion rules before any data, as yes/no flags (pp.588, 591).
2. Before the measured part, a short commitment by the player to answer carefully plus a plain line that answers are
   checked; no reward promises (p.590).
3. Keep every questionnaire short; place what matters early (p.579).
4. In the published numbers, run each result with and without flagged players and say so (p.593).
5. A watching virtual figure lowered carelessness on a web page (Ward & Pond 2015); in a room it would also change
   the experiment, so only where the original had an experimenter present (inference).

## Part 2. Olson et al., "Open-label choice blindness" (BIAL report abstract, 2 pages)

Takeaway: choice-blindness attitude change happens even when people know their answers were altered; it stays on
the altered items and does not spread.
- 147 students; measures of experiential avoidance, depressive symptoms, life satisfaction; three conditions:
  control (explain 3 of their own items), choice blindness (items covertly moved toward less agreement), and
  non-deceptive choice blindness (same, knowing they were moved); all re-measured one week later (p.1).
- Result: both choice-blindness conditions lowered agreement with the altered items, so "deception is not necessary
  for this kind of attitude change"; no spread to the total score, depression or life satisfaction (p.1).
- Not in this text: how many noticed (our earlier "80% missed" stays unverified), effect sizes, the one-week
  numbers. Published as Artenie, Olson et al. 2023, Psychology of Consciousness, doi:10.1037/cns0000372 (p.1).
Take: 1. An open-label version exists, an option for a no-deception room (inference: needs the full paper first).
2. Show players only item-level change, never "your personality changed". 3. A one-week return visit is a known
design. Open item: the journal article (APA, paywalled) for detection rates and effect sizes.

## Part 3. McKay, Hall, Strandberg et al. (2025), Religion, Brain & Behavior, published version (CC BY)

Takeaway: about half of reversed religious-attitude answers are accepted and justified, the reversal moves later
ratings strongly (d = 1.09), and religious people detect no better than atheists.
- Design: in person, iPad app (Self-Transforming Survey); 12 items, each marked with a finger on a 0-100 line, with
  an "edit" button and a practice item; done alone (p.5). Then the four target items are shown one by one: read
  aloud, say where the mark is, explain it. Two of four, chosen at random, were moved across the midpoint into 23-35
  or 65-77; hesitating participants were reminded they could edit; the assistant was blind to which were moved
  (p.6). Detection = any edit (p.7). Study 3 adds a paper survey of the 4 targets among 8 fillers; preregistered.
- N: 139 -> 130; 105 -> 98; 133 -> 110 (aimed 150; 21 lost to app faults, 2 refused consent after debrief) (p.4).
- Detection: Study 1, 80% missed at least one, 39% both; 105 of 260 (40%) corrected. Study 2: 68%, 29%, 101 of 196
  (52%). Study 3: 74%, 39%, 96 of 220 (44%) (p.7). Per item .25 to .69, in row order .42 .30 .56 .35 .40 .38 .69
  .58 .25 .59 .26 .58 (Table 5, p.8; order checked on the rendered page, the text layer scrambles it).
- After-effect (Study 3): moved items shifted 17.72 vs 1.21 points, t(109) = 8.7, d = 1.09; fully blind (n = 43)
  31.74 vs 4.42, d = 1.71; detected both (n = 29) no change, d = 0.08 (p.10).
- Extremity: each point from the midpoint raises detection odds about 9%; extremity 10 vs 30 means over five times
  the odds (p.9). Detectors stay anchored: b = 5.46 (p.10), "manipulated values can exert gravitational pull even
  when overtly rejected" (p.12); weak in Study 3.
- Atheists vs religious: blind 21 of 30 vs 21 of 28, OR 0.78, p = .67 (p.10).
- Ethics: second consent after debrief; online work recruits Prolific users who pre-agreed to deception; "the more
  powerful the paradigm, the more ethically problematic"; no delayed measure (p.14).
Check against our notes (own-experiments-now.md, lines 176-180, from the authors' OSF manuscript): every number
compared (Ns, the three detection rates, d values, OR, anchoring, extremity, Table 5, footnotes) is identical. Our
note needs no correction; the published version only adds dates (received 17 Aug 2024, online 24 Sep 2025) and
page numbers.
Take: 1. Copy the mechanics exactly: own mark on 0-100, review screen, 2 of 4 moved into 23-35/65-77, edit always
available, any edit = detected; only then can the player's result be set beside 40-52% corrected. 2. Expect
detection to rise with conviction; prefer items players hold moderately (inference). 3. Measure the after-effect
with a second rating among fillers. 4. Debrief, second consent, right to withdraw. 5. Religious items are
special-category data (inference: a question for the ethics notes, not settled here).

## Part 4. Pagulayan, Keeker, Wixon, Romero & Fuller, "User-centered design in games" (Microsoft Game Studios)

Year: the draft says "(in press)" for Jacko & Sears (Eds.), Handbook of HCI, Erlbaum; its latest source is a June
2001 personal communication. Saved as pagulayan-2003 (the Handbook's first edition is 2003 in OpenAlex).
Takeaway: separate "can they operate it" (small usability tests, think-aloud) from "do they like it" (larger on-site
playtests with forced-choice plus "why"), and guard the second against priming, halo and unrepresentative players.
- Playtest lab: up to 17 at once, partitions, headphones, questionnaires on screen (p.19). Usability tests cover
  only about the first hour of play (p.20).
- RITE: 1 to 3 players per build, fixes made before the next player; Age of Empires II tutorial: 7 builds across
  16 players, errors and failures fell to zero (pp.22, 24). Risks: unclear fixes create new problems (p.24).
- Natural play: open task "Play the game as if you were at home" (p.24); benchmark playtests use one fixed protocol,
  stop once for first impressions, then play on (p.30).
- Priming: in the Munch's Oddysee camera test (at least 25 target players, after one hour of play) camera questions
  came last, because naming the camera made silent players criticise it; 46% said too little control; 43% had
  criticised it before being asked (pp.27-28).
- Repeat players: the MechCommander 2 follow-up used 25 new people ("not the same people"): "too easy" fell from 33%
  to 13%, "not exciting" to 3% (pp.28-29). Deep gameplay deliberately reuses one cohort 1-3 times a month for content
  past the first hour; "recon" with professional gamers is not consumer feedback (pp.30-31).
- Halo: an uncomfortable controller dragged down ratings of every game on that system (p.17); Blood Wake: fun was
  not rated until the boat controls were fixed, since the two could not be separated (p.33).
- Experiment: within-subjects aiming test, 73% liked auto-aim vs 38% manual (p.33). Numbers need context: a
  comparison database answers "is 70% good?" (p.26). Focus groups: 1-3 per topic vs 4-12 advised, and the survey
  just taken colours the talk (p.32). Tutorials: learn by doing, "explanation is boring" (p.16).
Take: 1. General questions first, the measured thing last; count unprompted mentions before asking (pp.27-28).
2. Fresh players for each check; returning players are a separate, flagged group. 3. Fix comfort and controls before
judging any feeling; in a headset discomfort colours every answer (p.17, inference for VR). 4. RITE for the
practice part: 1-3 testers per build. 5. The reveal needs a comparison base (original + other players), as p.26.

## Part 5. Herrmann, Thoeni & Gaechter (2008), Science 319:1362-1367, with the SOM

Takeaway: one public goods game with punishment, run identically in 16 university pools; people everywhere punished
some cooperators, and where they did so most, punishment stopped raising cooperation.
- Design: 1120 undergraduates, 16 pools, 15 countries; groups of 4, 20 tokens, 0.4 per token to each member; P
  condition: 0-10 deduction points, each costs the punisher 1 and the target 3; 10 periods, fixed groups (p.1363);
  45 of 53 sessions ran N then P (SOM 5).
- Samara (Samara State University): 152 people (72 in the P-then-N order), 1 Guilder = RUB 0.2, 19.0% knew someone
  in the session, age 20.0, 53.9% women. Minsk (Belarusian National Technical University): 68 (36), BYR 17, 5.2%,
  age 19.8, 2.9% women (Table S2, SOM 11; checked on the rendered page).
- Mean contribution with punishment: Minsk 12.9, Samara 11.7 of 20 (range Athens 5.7 to Boston 18) (Fig. 2A, p.1364).
  Without: Minsk 10.5, Samara 9.7 (Fig. 3, p.1365); Table S9 gives Samara 9.9 and 11.8; rise N to P: Minsk +22.8%
  (p = .015), Samara +19.5% (p = .069); period 1: Minsk 12.8 -> 11.8, Samara 10.8 -> 10.8 (SOM 33).
- Antisocial punishment: Minsk 12th and Samara 13th of 16, lowest Boston, highest Muscat (Fig. 1, p.1363).
- Earnings per period N -> P: Minsk 26.3 -> 20.0 (-23.9%), Samara 25.8 -> 17.8 (-30.8%) (Table S6, SOM 27).
- Punished low contributors raised giving by 0.247 tokens per point in Samara (p < .05), 0.123 in Minsk (n.s.);
  punished above-average contributors cut giving: Minsk -0.351 (p < .01), Samara -0.265 (p < .05) (Table S7, SOM 29).
  Revenge (antisocial punishment rising with punishment received last period): Samara 0.314 (p < .01), Minsk 0.171
  (n.s.) (Table S3B, SOM 23). Column alignment checked against the counts stated in the SOM text.
- Country scores: civic norms Russia 8.05, Belarus 6.91 (lowest in the sample); rule of law -0.88 and -1.23
  (lowest) (Table S1, SOM 6). No order effects in Samara, Minsk, St. Gallen (p > 0.27); the three ex-Soviet pools do
  not differ pairwise (p > .23) (SOM 5). In some Samara sessions groups changed between N and P by a fault; the
  session was used as the unit (S78, SOM 38). Gintis's commentary groups Russia and Belarus with high antisocial
  punishment (Science 319:1346).
Running one experiment across cultures (SOM 3-4, 18-19): instructions written in German and English, translated by
a native speaker, translated back by another, "fine-tuned" until rules read the same; the same control questions,
and nobody starts until everyone answers all correctly; one author ran 43 of 53 sessions and trained local
experimenters on one script (he speaks Russian and Arabic); silence, partitions, about 21 per session; questions
about purpose are not answered; z-Tree everywhere with translated screens; money counted in "Guilders" so numbers
look alike, converted at a rate set from local student income and student hourly wages so stakes match; show-up fee,
25-Guilder lump sum in P, private cash payment.
Take: 1. Copy the protocol discipline: one script, back-translated text, comprehension checks that must be passed.
2. Points need a stated conversion logic; no money is a declared deviation. 3. For Russian-speaking players the
Samara and Minsk figures are the direct comparison in the reveal. 4. Log revenge and punished cooperators'
reactions; both are automatic. 5. Say who the original sample was (Minsk: technical university, 2.9% women).

## Part 6. Wu, Chen, Xia, Wang & Li (2023), Alzheimer's & Dementia 19(S18):e077166, poster (G3)

Takeaway: a company's three one-minute WeChat games for cognitive screening drew a huge, mostly young audience.
- "more than 11 million users have completed G3 tests" by 31 Aug 2022: confirmed as the poster's own claim, with no
  method or independent check; 85.38% were under 40, 2.05% over 60 (p.1).
- Over 45: education low/middle/university 2.93%/32.4%/59.65%; completion 92.28%; over 80 above 86% (p.1).
- Games "Number Ordering", "Species Sorting", "Gold finding", adaptive difficulty, a six-domain report; login asks
  gender, age, education (p.1, Fig. 2). How it spread: not stated (pages 2-3 are figures only).
- All authors work for Shanghai Bestcovered Ltd. Our earlier note quotes the same 92.28% for "users aged 50 and older
  (71 179)" from Li et al. 2023; this poster says "over 45": the company's two abstracts disagree on the age band.
Take: 1. Three one-minute tasks plus an instant personal report is the reach pattern. 2. A self-test about yourself
pulls the curious young, whoever it was built for. 3. Cite 11 million only as "company-reported".

## Part 7. Gavrilov, "Toloka as a source of respondents" (Sotsiologiya: 4M 2021 no. 53, pp.165-203, Russian)

Takeaway: Toloka gives fast, cheap Russian-speaking respondents of quality comparable to a panel and social-media
recruits, but only 35.7% pass one instructed-response check, and doubling pay does not change that.
- Dates: all three sources May-June 2020; an extra Toloka-only wave in summer 2022 (p.183); the issue is labelled
  2021 but reports 2022 data and July 2022 access dates (published late).
- Setup: SurveyMonkey link plus completion code; pay $0.03 then $0.06; Russian language; desktop only; top 80% of
  workers; low captcha rate; 7-day deferred acceptance; 3600 s per task page (p.179). The phone filter leaked: 7% of
  Android workers could not finish (p.181).
- Complete N: social media 189 (unpaid, 72% women, mean age 20.3); OMI panel 338 (at most 75 roubles); Toloka 1370
  (51%, 36.7); Toloka 2022 830 (43%, 39.5) (Table 2, p.183). Over 10 times cheaper than the panel; 600 workers in
  2 h 23 min (p.184).
- Completion: social 44.1% (189/428), panel 94.4% (338/358), Toloka 91.8% (1370/1493); mean/median time 14:59/12:54,
  14:36/11:43, 11:14/10:20; straightliners (same column in at least 4 of 12 grids) 1.6%, 13.3%, 8.1% (Table 3,
  p.189); straightliners took 7.5 vs 12.5 min (p.188). Optional open comment: 7.9%, 15.1%, 9.8% (2022) answered;
  mean words 21.1, 5.3, 7.3 (Table 4, p.189).
- Attention item (from Agley et al. 2022): "When you were at school in the senior grades, how acceptable was it for
  you to skip a lesson? Please ignore the answer options and choose the last option, that you did not go to school"
  (our translation) (pp.186-187). Result: 296 of 830 (35.7%) chose it (p.191). So our unverified "36%" is right, but
  it is one item, Toloka 2022 only, not compared with the other sources (the author says so, p.197).
- Attentive vs not: 13:54 vs 11:29 min; straightliners 6.1% vs 11.6%; open answers 10.8% vs 9.2% (n.s.); words 11.4
  vs 4.5 (Table 5, p.192).
- Pay $0.06 (232) vs $0.12 (598): attentive 30.4% vs 34.4%, "increasing the reward does not affect how attentively
  respondents answer" (our translation), chi2 = 1.38, p = .24; +1.5 min; open answers 4.3% -> 11.9%; straightliners
  14.2% -> 7.9% (Table 6, pp.191-195). Error in the paper: 30.4% and 34.4% cannot average to the stated 35.7% total
  (weighted, about 33.3%); treat the per-pay shares with caution.
- Toloka cannot filter by approval rate (p.198) and cannot block past participants (our Chapkovski note).
Take: 1. Plan for roughly two thirds failing a single instructed item in a Russian-speaking online pool; fix the
rule beforehand. 2. Higher pay buys time and fewer straight lines, not attention. 3. Device filters leak: check the
device ourselves. 4. One code per version on the final page ended support requests (p.181).

## Part 8. Experimental Psychology (Russia) 2022, vol. 15 no. 2: the six VR papers (pp.4-81)

All but Krylova are funded by one Ministry of Education task on "high-level VR" (mostly MSUPE/Smolensk authors). All use
Unity programs and pre/post tests; groups are not said to be randomised except in Krylova; no paper measures
simulator sickness with a questionnaire.
(a) Barabanschikov & Selivanov, pp.4-19, the Vive-vs-monitor study. HTC Vive: 2880x1600, 90 Hz, 110 degrees,
  base stations, two controllers, life-size figures to walk round and cut; 15-23 min (p.7).
  - Study 1 (mental states): 73 in Vive (41 women, 18-25, humanities; 54 had never used a headset) across three
    programs (40 perpendiculars theorem, 23 second-order surfaces, 10 underwater park) vs 46 at monitors (32 women,
    17-25) with the perpendiculars program; Kulikova "Actual state" and mood scales before, right after, and after
    15 min rest (pp.7-8). Vive: "high" activation 9% -> 44%, low 27% -> 14%; elevated excitement 44% -> 90%; still
    raised after 15 min (pp.8-9). Monitors: activation fell, no other change (p.9). Smaller changes in people who had
    used headsets before (p.8).
  - Study 2 (creativity): 21 in Vive (13 women, 18-20) vs 18 at monitors (15 women), same "second-order surfaces"
    program; Johnson questionnaire, Torrance pictures, two Rorschach blots a day before and right after (pp.10-11).
    Vive: all three tests rose; monitors: Johnson and Torrance by Wilcoxon only (p.12). ANOVA on Johnson only (the
    rest non-normal): stage F = 40.15, group F = 0.00, interaction F(1,37) = 8.01, p = .007 (Table 1, p.13).
  - Side effects: none recorded in the studies, but in the discussion: "during calibration, when the user sees
    himself chest-deep in the ground, above the horizon line and so on, within a few seconds many participants feel
    slight nausea and light dizziness" (our translation) (p.15).
(b) Bakhchina & Strizhova, pp.59-69: 16 students (8 women, 18-22), within-subjects, order of topic and condition
  counterbalanced; two 10-min lessons, Vive vs monitor; chest-belt ECG (Zephyr), 5 min eyes-closed rest around each
  (pp.62-64). HRV showed equal activation in both; no acute stress (p.64). Correct answers median 0.73 VR vs 0.66
  monitor (W = 11, p = .05); difficulty higher in VR (p = .04). Contradiction: Table 1 rates VR as less new (median
  1 vs 2, p = .04, p.66) while the abstract and discussion say "newer".
(c) Anikina, Khoze & Strizhova, pp.20-36: 28 students (21 women, 17-25), Vive, 15 min, no control group; nonverbal
  originality (p <= .05) and uniqueness (p <= .01) rose; verbal scores did not (pp.26-28).
(d) Marinova, pp.49-58: 120 students (74 women, 18-54); Samsung Gear VR ("Oculus Samsung") on a Galaxy S10+, Ocean
  Rift dolphins scene chosen by vote, swivel chair, 20 min; screened beforehand for fear of immersion, prior VR and
  vestibular problems (pp.51-53). Spielberger-Khanin and Beck before/after: only the high-anxiety group improved
  (p < .05) (p.53). "A negative factor was that participants felt slight dizziness after the VR goggles" (our
  translation) (p.54).
(e) Pobokin & Selivanov, pp.37-48: monitors only (no headset), 105 pupils aged 16-17; test score 3.78 -> 5.72 with
  the program vs 3.96 -> 4.8 with a teacher, d = 1.37 vs 0.55 (p.43); groups split after a diagnostic test.
(f) Krylova & Vodyakha, pp.70-81: iPad, not a headset; 115 children aged 4-6 in 5 randomised groups.
Take: 1. Only (b) is a clean headset-vs-monitor comparison (within-subjects, counterbalanced), and it is n = 16;
none of these can show that a headset changes a classic effect. 2. Calibration that puts the view below the floor
causes nausea within seconds (p.15): start every room with the view at standing height (inference). 3. Ask about
fear of immersion and vestibular problems before entry, as (d) did. 4. Prior headset users react less: record VR
experience for every player.

## Part 9. Selivanov (2019), Experimental Psychology (Russia) 12(1):40-52, theory review with one VR study

Takeaway: a review of Brushlinsky's theory of thinking; the VR study is a summary of earlier work, not a new report.
- Headset: eMagin Z800 3D Visor; a 3D hint of the task parts could be handled while solving aloud (microsemantic
  protocol analysis); one-factor design; ages 20-35 and 60-65 (p.45).
- Results: wider search and up to three times more lateral ideas; "approximately 68% of participants did not solve
  the problem even after two hints in the virtual environment" (our translation) (p.45). Sample size: not given
  (details in Selivanov 2016, pp.90-96). Side effects: not mentioned.
- Other summaries: after a biology VR program, thinking moved to mixed (43%) or directed (57%) level (Sorochinsky
  2013, pp.45-46); a short nyctophobia training VR sped simple reaction time and raised working memory (p.46).
- Inconsistency: the 2022 paper describes the same chain-puzzle study as 30 people aged 20-30 (20-35 a few lines
  on) and 20 aged 68-77, collaterals up 3 to 6 times (2022, p.10); this 2019 text says 20-35 and 60-65, up to 3 times.
Take: cite only as background; no numbers usable for a player-facing fact.

## Across the batch (inference)
- Ward & Meade, Gavrilov and Pagulayan agree: unsupervised players drift; plan flags and exclusions in advance,
  ask the specific question last, and keep fresh and repeat players apart.
- McKay and Herrmann both show that a re-creation is only comparable if the mechanics and the scoring rule are
  copied exactly (McKay: any edit = detected; Herrmann: one script, back-translation, passed control questions).
