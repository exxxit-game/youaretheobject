## Part 1 findings

### 1.1 Learned helplessness — the human effect, and the 2016 reversal of its explanation

- Hiroto 1974, "Locus of control and learned helplessness", J Exp Psychol 102(2):187-193. Read: full
  (open scan https://www.appstate.edu/~steelekm/classes/psy5300/Documents/Hiroto1974-learned-helplessness.pdf,
  library `hiroto-1974.txt`; the scan prints escape and inescapable groups both as "E", read from context).
  96 students, internals/externals at least 1 SD from the mean (James scale), 3 x 2 x 2 design.
  Pretreatment: "30 unsignaled 5-sec. trials" of a 3,000-Hz tone "at 110 db", mean gap 20 s; a red button
  that stops the tone (escape) or does nothing (inescapable), or no pretreatment. Test: a sliding-knob
  "human analogue to the 2-way shuttle box", "18 signaled 10-sec. trials", a 5-s red warning light first.
  Result (p. 189): the inescapable group "failed to escape the aversive stimulus on over 50% of the 18
  trials, while E Ss failed on 13% and NP Ss failed on 11%"; latency F(2, 84) = 12.38, p < .01. Externals
  slower than internals, F(1, 84) = 6.58, p < .05; "chance" instructions also made people more helpless.
  Time for the participant: about 12 min pretreatment + about 9 min test (from the trial counts and gaps).
- Hiroto & Seligman 1975, "Generality of learned helplessness in man", JPSP 31(2):311-327. Read: full
  (open scan .../Hiroto&Seligman1975-learned-helplessness.pdf, library `hiroto-seligman-1975.txt`).
  96 students, four experiments of 24 (8 per group). Tone 90 dB, 45 trials; or three Levine
  discrimination problems of 10 trials with a fixed "correct/incorrect" schedule (insoluble); tests:
  shuttlebox (20 trials) or 20 five-letter anagrams with one hidden letter order (3-4-2-5-1).
  A mismatch inside the paper: the abstract says "Learned helplessness was found with all four
  experiments", the Results say "The Cog.-Cog. experiment did not show significant effects although the
  results were in the predicted direction." The purely cognitive version (puzzles, then anagrams) is
  the weakest of the four.
- Maier & Seligman 2016, "Learned helplessness at fifty: Insights from neuroscience", Psychol Rev
  123(4):349-367, doi:10.1037/rev0000033, PMC4920136. Read: full (PMC; library `maier-seligman-2016.txt`).
  Abstract: "the original theory got it backwards. Passivity in response to shock is not learned. It is
  the default, unlearned response to prolonged aversive events"; "This passivity can be overcome by
  learning control". Caveat from the same paper: the circuit (prefrontal cortex inhibiting the dorsal
  raphe) was mapped in rats; on humans, "studies using methods that allow the measurement of neural
  activity are quite recent and few in number", and the treatment ideas are marked "we now speculate".
  On the human studies the paper reports people from the yoked group saying "nothing worked so why try?"
  and that the same pattern followed "solvable and unsolvable anagrams" instead of noise.
- Sound status: the behavioural effect in small 1970s samples (n = 8-32 per cell) is well known; a modern
  preregistered human replication: see below if found. The "passivity is the default, control is learned"
  line is an animal-neuroscience reinterpretation, not a human finding.
- Room fit, Hiroto 1974: clear task, published procedure, about 20 min (too long; the 1975 version has
  45 pretreatment trials). Hard limits: 110 dB (and 90 dB) through earphones is above safe listening
  levels and cannot be copied; it needs an aversive stimulus replaced, which changes the experiment.
  The insoluble-problem variant (Levine cards then anagrams) avoids noise but is the version that was
  not significant in 1975, and anagrams depend on the player's language. Deceiving a player into failure
  also needs a careful debrief (the paper paid $2 and debriefed).

### 1.2 Locus of control (Rotter 1966) — not read; one tested link

- Rotter 1966, Psychol Monogr 80(1):1-28, doi:10.1037/h0092976 (Crossref) — not read (no copy fetched in this run). What was read: in Hiroto 1974
  (full, above) externals were slower to escape than internals, latency F(1, 84) = 6.58, p < .05, and the
  "chance" instruction made people more helpless than the "skill" instruction ("You are potentially in
  control of the situation"). Being told the outcome is up to you changed behaviour in the same session.
  No modern replication of that instruction effect was checked.

### 1.3 Self-efficacy (Bandura) — belief follows success more than it causes it

- Bandura 1977 — not read. Sitzmann & Yeo 2013, Personnel Psychology, read: abstract only (Consensus):
  within-person self-efficacy/performance correlation .23 but "weak and nonsignificant (ρ = .06) when
  controlling for the linear trajectory"; past performance to self-efficacy .40; "self-efficacy is
  primarily a product of past performance rather than the driving force affecting future performance".
  Yeo & Neal 2006 (abstract only): within a person, higher task self-efficacy went with lower performance.
- Status: contested (Bandura replied in 2012); for the game it means confidence is best built by a real
  success the player sees, not by being told he is able. Unverified beyond abstracts.

### 1.4 Illusion of control and depressive realism — the original did not hold up

- Alloy & Abramson 1979, J Exp Psychol Gen 108(4):441-485, doi:10.1037/0096-3445.108.4.441 (Crossref-checked). Read: full
  (library `alloy-abramson-1979.txt`). Task: 40 trials, press or not press a button, a green light comes
  on or not; then judge "degree of control" 0-100; then 10 money trials (a quarter per light).
  Exp. 1: 96 students; problems with 25%, 50%, 75% control. Summary (p. 441): "Depressed
  students' judgments of contingency were surprisingly accurate in all four experiments." Non-depressed
  students "overestimated the degree of contingency" when non-contingent outcomes were frequent.
  Session pay topped up to $2.50 for all.
- Dev, Moore, Johnson & Garrett 2022, "Sadder ≠ Wiser: Depressive Realism is not Robust to Replication",
  Collabra: Psychology, preregistered, https://osf.io/67agr/. Read: full (library
  `dev-moore-johnson-garrett-2022.txt`, preprint text "appeared in print in Collabra"). N = 246 MTurk +
  134 (136 in the method) undergraduates; 40-trial lightbulb task, zero (25%, 75%) and positive (75%)
  contingency. Abstract: "We found no evidence that depressive symptoms relate to illusory control or
  to overconfidence." Sample 1: depression did predict control bias, but the wrong way, "more severe
  depression is associated with greater overestimates of control"; Sample 2: F(3,110) = 0.821, p = .49.
  They quote the Moore & Fresco 2012 meta-analysis: studies with an objective standard of accuracy
  found weighted mean d = -.03 versus -.15 for those without (not read: the 2012 meta-analysis itself).
- What held: in both 1979 and 2022 people as a whole overestimate control when outcomes do not depend on
  them (Dev: "participants' overestimates of control are limited to the zero contingency conditions").
  What did not hold: that depressed people are more accurate. Status: the "sadder but wiser" claim
  should not be shown to players as a finding.
- Room fit: the Alloy-Abramson button-and-light task is 40 trials plus 10 money trials, a few minutes,
  fully published procedure; the reveal ("you felt in control of a light that ignored you") is the
  general illusion of control, which held. The depression part must not be used (screening players
  for depression is a clinical step the game should not take).

### 1.5 Status quo bias and defaults — strong on choices, weak as an explanation of a life

- Jachimowicz, Duncan, Weber & Johnson 2019, Behavioural Public Policy 3(2):159-186 — not read (search
  results only: 58 studies, N = 73,675; the authors' summary says defaults shift choices by 0.63-0.68 SD;
  unverified). Library has hindriks-douven-2018 and weijers-2014 (status quo bias in thought experiments),
  not reopened here.
- Status: defaults move one-off choices a lot; nothing found links them to people underusing abilities.

### 1.6 Effort is felt as a cost — the best-supported base fact

- David, Vassena & Bijleveld 2024, "The unpleasantness of thinking", Psychol Bull 150(9):1070-1093,
  doi:10.1037/bul0000443. Read: abstract only (PubMed): 170 studies, 4,670 people, 29 countries; "a strong
  positive association between mental effort and negative affect"; "mental effort is inherently aversive".
  No number taken (the abstract gives none). Present bias / delay discounting: not searched in this run.
- Status: that effort feels bad, everywhere, is well supported; that people therefore avoid it unless it
  pays is the standard model. This, not a hidden flaw of character, is the plainest answer to "why the
  couch": the couch costs nothing now.

### 1.7 Hedonic adaptation — the lottery-winner story half held

- Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman 1978, "Lottery winners and accident victims: Is happiness relative?",
  JPSP 36(8):917-927, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.36.8.917 (PubMed 690806). Read: NOT read (no open copy
  found); its numbers below are as Lindqvist et al. 2020 report them. Paywall: for the owner.
- Lindqvist, Östling & Cesarini 2020, "Long-run effects of lottery wealth on psychological well-being",
  Rev Econ Stud 87(6):2703-2726, doi:10.1093/restud/rdaa006, open access. Read: full (library
  `lindqvist-2020.txt`). Preregistered; Swedish winners "5-22 years after a major lottery event".
  "an after-tax prize of $100,000 improves life satisfaction by 0.037 standard deviation (SD) units";
  "no evidence that the effect varies by years-since-win"; effects on happiness and mental health
  "smaller and not statistically" significant. On Brickman: "22 major lottery winners of the Illinois
  State Lottery" against 22 controls, "no statistically significant differences"; rescaled, 0.014 (SE
  0.025), close to Lindqvist's own happiness estimate (0.016).
- Status: held: momentary happiness barely moves with a windfall. Did not hold: that people fully adapt
  so that life satisfaction returns to where it was. The accident-victim half was not checked here.
- Bearing on "settling": adaptation explains why a gain stops feeling like one; it does not show that
  people underuse their abilities. Room fit: none (a survey of real winners, not a task).

### 1.8 Boredom and the disengaged mind — the "rather a shock than thinking" result replicated, its reason did not

- Wilson et al. 2014, "Just think: The challenges of the disengaged mind", Science 345(6192):75-77,
  doi:10.1126/science.1250830, PMC4330241 (author manuscript). Read: full (library `wilson-2014.txt`).
  11 studies; alone "for 6 to 15 min" with no phone. Study 10 (15 min, a button for a shock they had
  rated): "67% of men (12 of 18) gave themselves at least one shock during the thinking period", "25% of
  women (6 of 24)", counting only those who had said they would pay to avoid it. Planning what to think
  about did not help: "none reliably increased participants' enjoyment". Their line: "The untutored mind
  does not like to be alone with itself."
- Buttrick et al. 2019, JPSP 117(5):e71-e83, doi:10.1037/pspp0000198. Read: abstract only (PubMed):
  "2,557 participants from 12 sites in 11 countries"; "Participants randomly assigned to do something
  reported significantly greater enjoyment" than those told to think for pleasure, "in every country"
  (the enjoyment part replicated; the shock part was not in this study).
- Eder et al. 2022, Sci Rep, doi:10.1038/s41598-022-14775-w, PMC9253005. Read: full (library
  `eder-2022.txt`). Four experiments, N = 254: "Most participants in our studies opted to administer
  shocks to themselves (range: 63-89%), and proportions were substantially larger than in the original
  study (43%)". But they shocked themselves strongly even when a no-shock or mild option was there, and
  also when helped to think pleasant thoughts: "it seems that the self-infliction of pain was attractive
  for many participants, because they were curious".
- Fox et al. 2014, Front Psychol (commentary), read: abstract only: they "found very little support"
  in Wilson's data for the first and third claims.
- Status: held: people left with nothing to do prefer doing something, and many press a shock button.
  Did not hold as stated: that they shock themselves to escape their thoughts (curiosity explains it as
  well). A game must not say "you would rather hurt yourself than think".
- Room fit: a published 15-min procedure with one button; the shock cannot be given by a headset, and a
  replacement stimulus changes the experiment. Long empty waiting in VR is also a comfort and attention
  problem. Possible, not first choice.

### 1.9 Learned industriousness — effort can be learned to feel rewarding (mostly one lab's evidence)

- Eisenberger 1992, "Learned industriousness", Psychol Rev 99(2):248-267, doi:10.1037/0033-295X.99.2.248
  (PubMed 1594725). Read: full (copy https://homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2013/01/Learned-Industriousness.pdf,
  library `eisenberger-1992.txt`). A review. Key human design (Eisenberger & Leonard 1980, Exp. 3, as
  described there): students solved complex anagrams, simple anagrams, or were yoked (simple ones when the
  partner succeeded, "an unsolvable anagram" when the partner failed); "The complex-anagram group showed
  greater subsequent persistence than the other two groups on unsolvable perceptual problems". Also: those
  "previously been required to solve difficult anagrams cheated less". The paper itself separates this from
  "impossibly difficult tasks that produce failure and learned helplessness (Hiroto & Seligman, 1975)".
- Independent test: Bustamante, Davis & Marquez 2014, Int J Psychol Stud 6(4):12, doi:10.5539/ijps.v6n4p12,
  PMC4456027. Read: abstract only. 80 inactive young adults: "Findings do not support the Theory of
  Learned Industriousness nor generalization of effort across physical and mental domains."
- Status: the mirror image of learned helplessness (rewarded hard work raises later persistence) rests
  mainly on Eisenberger's own small studies; no meta-analysis or multi-lab replication found. Unverified as
  robust.
- Room fit: possible and attractive (hard vs easy anagrams, then time spent on an unsolvable task, about 10
  min), but language-bound (anagrams) and weakly replicated; the procedures of the 1980 primary paper were
  not read.

### 1.10 Self-determination theory — paying for a task lowers free-time play with it (held, moderate)

- Deci 1971, "Effects of externally mediated rewards on intrinsic motivation", JPSP 18(1):105-115.
  Read: full (scan https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/1971_Deci.pdf, OCR in library
  `deci-1971.txt`). Exp. I: 24 students, three sessions "of not more than 1 hour each" on 3 days; the
  Soma puzzle ("seven pieces of plastic"), four drawn configurations per session, 13-min limit each;
  session 2: experimental group "paid $1 for each configuration". Measure: the experimenter leaves "for
  8 minutes" on a pretext ("you may do whatever you like while I am gone"; magazines on the table) and
  times, through a one-way window, how long the person keeps working on the puzzle. Result: paid group
  minus control, session 3 vs 1, -77.6 s, "significance at the .10 level" (one-tailed). The founding
  result was weak.
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, Psychol Bull 125(6):627-668, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627. Read: full
  (copy https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf,
  library `deci-koestner-ryan-1999.txt`). "A meta-analysis of 128 studies"; "When all 92
  tangible-rewards studies were considered together, the effect was highly significant, d = -0.34 (CI =
  -0.39, -0.28)" on free-choice behaviour; "Positive feedback enhanced both free-choice behavior (d =
  0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31)". The rival meta-analysis by Cameron & Pierce (cited there)
  found smaller effects (free choice "d = -0.21") — a long dispute, not read on its own here.
- Status: held across ~100 studies: expected tangible rewards for an interesting task reduce later
  free-time engagement with it; informational praise raises it. Bearing on "giving up": people stop
  doing what they once liked when the reason to do it moves outside them.
- Room fit: strong. The 8-min "free choice" period while the experimenter is away is a hidden, automatic
  measure, and a 3D puzzle with controllers suits VR. Limits: the original spans three hour-long
  sessions (a one-session version is needed, which is a change and must be named as such); real money
  cannot be paid in the game, and in-game points are not the same reward. The positive-feedback arm
  (praise raises free-time play) needs no money.

### 1.11 Growth mindset — real but very small, and contested

- Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara 2018, Psychol Sci 29(4):549-571, doi:10.1177/0956797617739704.
  Read: full (author copy https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/sisk-et-al-2018-to-what-extent-and-under-which-circumstances-are-growth-mind-sets-important-to-academic-achievement-two.pdf;
  library `sisk-2018.txt`). Meta-analysis 1 (k = 273, N = 365,915): mindset-achievement correlation
  "r = .10, 95% confidence interval (CI) = [.08, .13]". Meta-analysis 2 (k = 43, N = 57,155):
  interventions "d = 0.08, 95% CI = [0.02, 0.14]". Conclusion: "the second meta-analysis demonstrated only
  a very small overall effect of mind-set interventions"; benefit only for "academically high-risk
  students and low-SES students".
- Yeager et al. 2019, Nature 573:364-369, doi:10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y, PMC6786290. Read: full (library
  `yeager-2019.txt`). Preregistered, 65 schools, 12,490 ninth-graders; two online sessions "approximately
  25 min each" about 20 days apart. Lower-achieving students: GPA +"0.10 grade points" ("standardized mean
  difference effect size of 0.11"); grades rose only where peer norms supported challenge-seeking.
- Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023 (online 2022), Psychol Bull, doi:10.1037/bul0000352. Read: full (author copy
  https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2022/Macnamara%20and%20Burgoyne%20(2022)%20-%20Do%20Growth%20Mindset%20Interventions%20Impact%20Students%E2%80%99%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf;
  library `macnamara-burgoyne-2023.txt`). 63 studies, N = 97,672: "d = 0.05, 95% CI = [0.02, 0.09], which
  was nonsignificant after correcting for potential publication bias"; best-quality 6 studies, N =
  13,571: "nonsignificant". Their reply (Psychol Bull 149(3-4):242-258, doi:10.1037/bul0000394, library
  `macnamara-burgoyne-2023-reply.txt`, read: full abstract and positions): "94% of growth mindset
  interventions included confounds"; authors with a financial incentive "two and a half times as likely to
  report positive effects".
- Burnette et al. 2023, Psychol Bull (doi not confirmed here), read: abstract only (Consensus record):
  targeted groups, high fidelity, academic achievement "d = 0.14, 95% CI [.06, .22]". Tipton et al. 2023
  (abstract only) re-analysed and found effects in at-risk groups. The dispute is open.
- Status: the belief that ability can grow is associated with achievement only weakly (r about .10); a
  short message teaching it moves grades by about a tenth of a standard deviation at best, for struggling
  students, where the surroundings support it. "You can do far more than you think" is not supported as
  a general effect of a message.

### 1.12 Stronger or contrary lines found

- Ego depletion ("willpower runs out") did not hold: Hagger et al. 2016, Perspect Psychol Sci 11(4):546-573,
  doi:10.1177/1745691616652873, library `hagger-2016.txt` (read: its results and conclusion): 24 replications, "d = 0.020,
  95% confidence interval: -0.089 to 0.130"; "if there is any effect, is close to zero". A game must not
  explain giving up by a used-up willpower tank.
- Maier & Seligman 2016 (1.1): passivity is the default and control is learned (animal evidence).
- Positive fantasies (Oettingen) and "praise backfires" (Brummelman 2016, abstract only in 2.6a's search):
  searched once, not read; unverified.

### 1.13 Russian-language research — helplessness as a trait, measured by questionnaires

- Vedeneeva 2009, thesis summary (supervisor D. A. Tsiring), Tomsk State University, "The motivational
  component of personal helplessness at different ages" (in Russian; title translated). Read: full
  (autoreferat, https://www.lib.tsu.ru/mminfo/000373515/000373515.pdf, library `vedeneeva-2009.txt`). The
  Russian line of the 1990s moved from helplessness as a state to "lichnostnaya bespomoshchnost" (personal
  helplessness), a stable trait with volitional, emotional, cognitive and motivational components (Tsiring;
  translated from the text). Method: questionnaires (ASQ, CASQ, Cattell 12/14/16PF) and cluster analysis;
  653 participants in total, 274 in the contrast groups (children, teenagers, students). Correlational, no
  experiment, no replication found.
- Leontiev (ed.) 2011, "Lichnostny potentsial: struktura i diagnostika" (Personal potential: structure and
  diagnostics; Smysl, 680 pp.) — personal potential as the capacity for self-regulation; found by search,
  not read (HSE copies did not open: psy.hse.ru unreachable). Unverified.
- Ukrainian-language research: not searched to the end in this run (see "not done").
- Status: the Russian work adds a vocabulary (personal helplessness vs self-reliance) but no tested
  procedure that a room could re-create.
